  A SWOT analysis example is the fastest way to see the framework working on a
  real business. The method is simple: list the strengths and weaknesses inside
  your business, list the opportunities and threats outside it, then turn those
  four lists into a few actions you can take this month. Below is a complete,
  fictional walkthrough for a small shop, with guiding questions for each
  quadrant and a timed plan that fits in about 30 minutes.


  ## SWOT analysis example: a small shop at a glance


  Harbor Hardware is a fictional independent hardware store in a mid-sized town.
  Owner Dana Ruiz runs it with three part-time employees. It has a loyal base of
  local contractors and DIY homeowners, but it competes with a big-box chain a
  few miles away and with online sellers that ship next day. Dana wants to grow
  sales without spending heavily on advertising.


  Small businesses are a large part of the economy. According to the [SBA Office
  of
  Advocacy](https://advocacy.sba.gov/2026/02/03/advocacy-releases-frequently-asked-questions-about-small-businesses-2026/),
  there are 36,207,130 small businesses in the United States, and they employ
  62.3 million people, or 45.9% of private sector workers. Most of those owners
  make strategic calls without a planning department, which is why a one-page
  framework is worth the half hour.

  ## The completed SWOT for Harbor Hardware


  The numbers below are hypothetical and included for illustration.


  ### Strengths (internal, helpful)


  - Staff who know the inventory well and can answer repair questions on the
  spot

  - Same-day key cutting and paint color matching that the big chain does not
  offer

  - A repeat base of about 60 local contractors who buy every month


  ### Weaknesses (internal, harmful)


  - A limited online catalog, so customers cannot check stock before they visit

  - Narrow weekend hours that miss DIY shoppers who work weekdays

  - A slow reorder process for mid-volume items, which causes frequent stockouts


  ### Opportunities (external, helpful)


  - Homeowners who want to learn basic repairs and would pay for a short
  hands-on class

  - Contractors who need a reliable local supplier for small jobs

  - Community groups that want space and workshops on practical skills


  ### Threats (external, harmful)


  - Big-box stores with lower prices on commodity items like fasteners and
  batteries

  - Online sellers that offer next-day delivery on the same products

  - A paint supplier that raised its wholesale prices last quarter


  ## How to run a SWOT analysis in 30 minutes


  Set a timer and work in a notebook or a shared document. Choose one time
  frame, such as the next 12 months, so every item is judged the same way.


  1. **Minutes 0–5, Strengths.** Ask what you do better than competitors and how
  a customer would know. Write every internal advantage you can think of, then
  mark the ones you can prove.

  2. **Minutes 5–10, Weaknesses.** Ask where customers leave, slow down, or
  complain. Be specific. 'Service could be better' is not an entry; 'checkout
  takes eight minutes at lunch' is.

  3. **Minutes 10–15, Opportunities.** Ask which outside trends, customer needs,
  or local partners you could use.

  4. **Minutes 15–20, Threats.** Ask what could hurt sales or margins in the
  next year. Include things you cannot control.

  5. **Minutes 20–25, Cut to three.** Keep only the three most important items
  in each quadrant, meaning the ones that would change a decision. Delete or
  merge the rest.

  6. **Minutes 25–30, Assign actions.** Pair the quadrants, as shown below, and
  fill in the action table.

  ## Guiding questions for each quadrant


  Use these questions to test each entry. If you cannot answer one, the item is
  probably a guess. For a longer question bank, SCORE's SWOT worksheet lists 75
  questions.


  ### Strengths


  - What do customers say we do best, in their own words?

  - Which skill or asset would be hard for a competitor to copy?

  - What would we lose if this strength disappeared?


  ### Weaknesses


  - Which complaint do we hear more than once?

  - Where do we lose sales we could have won?

  - Which problem do we keep fixing without solving?


  ### Opportunities


  - Which customer need do we see but do not yet serve?

  - Which local partner could send us regular business?

  - What change in our area would make the store more useful?


  ### Threats


  - Which competitor move would hurt us most?

  - Which cost could rise faster than our prices?

  - What outside change would make our current model outdated?


  Tie every item to evidence: a number, a customer quote, or a concrete example.
  That step is what separates a useful SWOT from a list of nice-sounding words.

  ## Turn the SWOT into action by pairing quadrants


  A SWOT becomes useful when you combine quadrants. [Atlassian's SWOT
  guide](https://www.atlassian.com/work-management/strategic-planning/swot-analysis)
  describes the same idea of matching internal factors with external ones. Here
  is how Harbor Hardware would do it:


  - **Strength + Opportunity:** Use what you do well to capture something new.
  Staff repair knowledge plus homeowner interest in basic repairs becomes a
  Saturday repair class.

  - **Weakness + Opportunity:** Fix a gap that an opportunity exposes. Narrow
  weekend hours plus weekday DIY shoppers suggests adding one late weekday
  opening.

  - **Strength + Threat:** Use a strength to blunt a threat. Same-day key
  cutting and paint matching are hard for online sellers to copy, so those
  services become the core of the store's pitch against next-day delivery.

  - **Weakness + Threat:** Shore up the gap a threat would exploit. A slow
  reorder process combined with a supplier price increase calls for a second
  supplier quote on the top 50 items.

  ## Common mistakes to avoid


  - **Vague entries.** 'Good location' tells you nothing. Add the reason it
  matters.

  - **Too many items.** Twelve bullets per quadrant hide your real priorities.

  - **Only one side of the grid.** A SWOT made only of strengths is a pitch, not
  an analysis.

  - **No owner or deadline.** An action without both is a wish.

  - **One and done.** Review the SWOT every quarter or after a major change,
  such as a new competitor or a supplier price shift.


  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### What does SWOT stand for?


  SWOT stands for strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats. Strengths
  and weaknesses are internal, so you have some control over them. Opportunities
  and threats are external.


  ### Who invented SWOT analysis?


  Accounts differ. The most common story credits Humphrey, who presented the
  idea in Zurich. According to [The Decision
  Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/management/swot-analysis), the
  original acronym was SOFT, which was promoted in Britain and evolved into
  SWOT. Other accounts point to case study work at Harvard Business School in
  the early 1950s, and the framework became widely popular in the 1980s.


  ### How many items should each quadrant have?


  Three to five is a practical range. That is enough to show the full picture
  and few enough to act on.


  ### What are the limits of a SWOT analysis?


  A SWOT is a snapshot, not a forecast. It lists factors without ranking them,
  and it can drift into wishful thinking if you skip the evidence. Use it to
  frame a decision, not to make the decision alone.


  ## Takeaway


  A good SWOT analysis example does two jobs: it shows the structure and it
  forces a decision. Set a 30-minute timer, keep three items per quadrant, tie
  each one to evidence, and turn the results into a table with owners and
  deadlines. Then revisit it next quarter and see what changed.
