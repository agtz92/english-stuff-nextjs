  So, what is Kwanzaa? It is a seven-day holiday celebrated mainly in the United
  States from December 26 to January 1. Each day focuses on one of seven
  principles, and families mark the week by lighting a candle, talking about
  that day’s value, and sharing a meal together at the end.


  The short answer to what is Kwanzaa: Kwanzaa is a cultural celebration rooted
  in African American and broader African heritage, built around family,
  community, and self-improvement. It was created in 1966, and it is generally
  described as secular rather than religious. Below, we cover where the name
  comes from, what each principle means, how the symbols work, and how people
  take part.


  ## What is Kwanzaa celebrating?


  Kwanzaa is organized around the Nguzo Saba, the Swahili name for seven guiding
  principles. The holiday asks households to reflect on how they treat one
  another and how they contribute to their community. Each evening, a family
  gathers, lights one candle, and discusses the principle for that day.


  The week closes with a feast called Karamu and a ritual pouring from a unity
  cup. Gifts are part of many families’ routines, but the emphasis is on
  reflection, shared time, and small, practical habits you can carry into the
  new year.


  ## Where the name comes from


  Understanding what is Kwanzaa starts with the name. The word comes from
  kwanza, a Swahili word meaning “first.” According to [Encyclopaedia
  Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Kwanzaa), Maulana Karenga
  borrowed the word from the Swahili phrase matunda ya kwanza, meaning “first
  fruits,” and added an extra a. That extra letter gave the name seven letters,
  one for each of the seven children present at an early celebration. Britannica
  also notes that Kwanzaa is not itself a Swahili word. The name was shaped for
  the holiday rather than taken from a single existing term.


  You may also hear the greeting “Habari gani?” during the week. It is Swahili
  for “What’s the news?” The person answering names the principle of the day, so
  the exchange doubles as a quick review of the seven values.

  ## Who created Kwanzaa, and why


  Maulana Karenga, a professor and chair of the Department of Africana Studies
  at California State University, Long Beach, created Kwanzaa in 1966. He
  described it as an effort at cultural recovery and reconstruction. The project
  came in the wake of the 1965 Watts Rebellion in Los Angeles, a period when
  many Black Americans were debating identity, history, and community power.
  [BlackPast](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/kwanzaa-1966/)
  places the holiday in that historical context.


  Anyone trying to grasp what is Kwanzaa will find the origin story useful.
  Karenga’s own account is available on [his official Kwanzaa
  page](https://maulanakarenga.org/kwanzaa/), which lays out the principles and
  the holiday’s purpose from the founder’s perspective. Read it alongside
  independent sources such as encyclopedias and museums to get the fullest
  picture.

  The black candle sits in the center of the kinara. The red candles sit to its
  left and represent the past, while the green candles to its right stand for
  the future.


  ## How families celebrate the week


  A typical household follows a simple routine that repeats each evening:


  1. Set up the mkeka before December 26 and place the kinara, mazao, muhindi,
  and unity cup on it.

  2. Each night, gather the family and have a child light the candle for that
  day.

  3. Discuss the principle. Some families read a short story, while others ask
  each person to share an example.

  4. Share a meal or a small activity that connects to the principle.

  5. On December 31, hold the Karamu feast, which is usually the week’s largest
  gathering. Many families invite friends and extended relatives, and the unity
  cup is used for the libation ritual.


  Karamu is the feast that closes the holiday. Families often share food with a
  Kwanzaa theme, and the gathering is designed to be inclusive, so guests of
  different backgrounds are common.

  ## Is Kwanzaa a religious holiday?


  No. Kwanzaa is generally described as a secular, cultural holiday, and that is
  how [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Kwanzaa) presents it. People
  of many faiths, and people with no religious affiliation, celebrate it.


  The founder’s framing has changed over time. In a 1997 book, Karenga wrote
  that “Kwanzaa was not created to give people an alternative to their own
  religion or religious holiday.” That statement speaks to a long-running
  debate. For many people asking what is Kwanzaa for the first time, the
  religious question comes early. Because the holiday falls in the same season
  as Christmas, some people have treated it as an alternative to Christmas.
  Others see it as a cultural practice that sits alongside whatever a family
  already observes. Households decide for themselves how it fits into their
  traditions.


  ## Can anyone celebrate Kwanzaa?


  Yes, anyone can take part, although the holiday was created with African
  American communities in mind. Respectful participation looks like this:


  - Ask a friend or colleague how their family observes it, and let them lead
  the conversation.

  - Learn the seven principles and the basic meaning of the symbols before you
  join a celebration.

  - Treat Kwanzaa as a cultural practice, not a costume or a themed party.

  - Be cautious with pre-packaged kits. Read what is inside before you call the
  result a tradition.

  - If you are invited to a Karamu, bring a dish or a small gift, and follow the
  host’s lead.

  - Ask before sharing photos or details of private family moments.


  ## How many people celebrate Kwanzaa?


  There is no single agreed number, and published estimates vary widely. A 2009
  estimate cited in encyclopedia sources put the number of U.S. adherents
  between 500,000 and 2 million. National Today cites a Public Policy Polling
  figure of 12.5 million Americans. Those numbers differ because surveys ask
  different questions, sample different groups, and run in different years.
  Someone who lights a candle with family may not identify as a Kwanzaa
  celebrant, and a yes-or-no question can produce a very different answer than a
  question about household practices.


  When you see a participation figure, check who conducted the survey, when it
  ran, and how the question was worded.

  ## Kwanzaa beyond the United States


  Most coverage focuses on the United States, where the holiday is most visible.
  Outside the United States, what is Kwanzaa looks much the same in structure.
  It is also marked in other diaspora communities, including parts of the
  Caribbean, where families adapt the principles and rituals to local life. The
  structure of seven days and seven principles stays consistent, while the food,
  music, and local customs vary from place to place.

  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### What is Kwanzaa, and when does it start and end?


  Kwanzaa begins on December 26 and ends on January 1. Each of the seven days is
  dedicated to one principle, and the celebration is designed to be observed
  over the full week.


  ### Who created Kwanzaa?


  Maulana Karenga, a professor at California State University, Long Beach,
  created Kwanzaa in 1966. He presented it as a way to recover and reconstruct
  African American cultural practices.


  ### Is Kwanzaa a religious holiday?


  Kwanzaa is generally described as secular. People of different faiths and of
  no faith celebrate it, and households decide how it fits with their other
  traditions.


  ### What does “Habari gani” mean?


  “Habari gani?” is a Swahili greeting that means “What’s the news?” During
  Kwanzaa, the person answering names the principle of the day.


  ### What is the Karamu feast?


  Karamu is the communal feast held on December 31, the sixth day of Kwanzaa.
  Families often share food with a Kwanzaa theme and invite friends and extended
  family.


  ## The takeaway


  If you came here asking what is Kwanzaa, the takeaway is simple: it is a
  seven-day, home-centered holiday created in 1966 by Maulana Karenga. Its seven
  principles, from Umoja to Imani, give each day a theme, and its candles, mat,
  and unity cup give the week its rhythm. Whether you celebrate or simply want
  to understand it, start with the principles, read the founder’s account next
  to independent sources, and treat the holiday with the same care you would
  give any family tradition. At 3minread, we’ll keep this guide current as the
  season approaches.
