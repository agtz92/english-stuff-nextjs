  Kanban vs Scrum comes down to one question: does your team work in fixed
  cycles, or does work arrive and flow continuously? Scrum organizes work into
  time-boxed Sprints with defined roles and a protected scope. Kanban visualizes
  tasks on a board, limits how much is in progress, and lets priorities change
  at any time. Neither method is better across the board. The right choice
  depends on three things: your team size, your delivery rhythm, and how often
  priorities shift. We compare both on those criteria below and give a verdict
  for four common team types.


  ## What Scrum and Kanban Actually Are


  Scrum is defined in the [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/), last updated
  in November 2020. It names three accountabilities: the Developers, the Product
  Owner, and the Scrum Master. Work happens in Sprints, which
  [ones.com](https://ones.com/blog/scrum-vs-kanban-comparison-agile-methodologies/)
  describes as time-boxed periods that usually last one to four weeks.


  Kanban is a method for making work visible. Teams map their tasks on a board,
  set limits on work in progress, and pull new items in as capacity opens up. It
  has no required roles, and it assumes work is continuous. As the [Scrum
  Alliance
  explains](https://resources.scrumalliance.org/Article/kanban-scrum-teams),
  changes in a Kanban workflow can happen at any time. Scrum.org lists four core
  Kanban practices and four key flow metrics in its [Kanban
  resources](https://www.scrum.org/scrum-kanban).

  ## How Team Size Changes the Answer


  Team size is the criterion most people search for first, and the sources do
  not agree on the numbers. ones.com says Scrum works well for cross-functional
  teams of 5 to 9 members, while Kanban works at any size. Nira recommends Scrum
  for teams of no more than 10 people, ideally in the same location. ThinkLouder
  argues that Scrum scales more easily at 6 or more people, while Kanban's
  simplicity wins at 3 or fewer. Mike Cohn describes the ideal Scrum team as a
  'two-pizza team' in his [Mountain Goat Software
  article](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/when-kanban-is-the-better-choice).
  The Scrum Guide sets no number at all. It says that if a Scrum Team becomes
  too large, it should consider reorganizing into multiple cohesive Scrum Teams.


  Here is the tie-breaker we use. Team size is a rough filter, not a verdict.
  Scrum tends to fit teams of about 4 to 9 people who share one goal. Below
  three people, Kanban's light structure usually makes more sense. Above nine,
  either split into several Scrum teams or move to Kanban. If the other two
  criteria point the opposite way, let them win. This is our rule of thumb, not
  an industry standard.

  ## Delivery Rhythm: A Worked Example


  Delivery rhythm often matters more than headcount. Consider two five-person
  app teams.


  Team A ships a small update every week as soon as a feature passes review. For
  this team, Kanban fits. Each finished item moves to release on its own
  schedule, with no need to wait for a sprint boundary. A one-week Sprint would
  add planning, review, and retrospective meetings every five working days,
  which can eat a large share of a week's development time.


  Team B releases a bigger version every two weeks, so its work naturally
  batches. Scrum fits well here. The Sprint length matches the release, the
  Sprint Review becomes a checkpoint before shipping, and the Sprint Goal gives
  stakeholders a clear promise for the next two weeks.


  The lesson is simple: match the framework's cadence to your release cadence,
  not the other way around.


  ## Mid-Sprint Priority Changes: What to Do


  Scrum protects the Sprint, but it does not freeze the business. The Scrum
  Guide allows the Product Owner to cancel a Sprint if the Sprint Goal becomes
  obsolete. Here is a step-by-step approach when an urgent request lands on day
  four of a ten-day Sprint:


  1. Check whether it is a real emergency. Does it block a live product or a
  major customer? If not, it goes to the backlog for the next Sprint.

  2. Bring it to the Product Owner. They own priorities and decide whether it
  outranks current work.

  3. If it does outrank the work, swap it in. Remove Sprint Backlog items of
  similar size and tell the team and stakeholders what changed.

  4. If the Sprint Goal no longer makes sense, the Product Owner can cancel the
  Sprint. Plan the next one right away.

  5. Review it in your next retrospective. If urgent requests show up every
  Sprint, your team may need Kanban-style flow or a separate support lane.


  In Kanban, the same request goes into the backlog column. The team checks it
  against the work-in-progress limit, and if it jumps the queue, something else
  waits. That is why Kanban suits high-interruption work better. If you want to
  see how these requests fit into a wider process, our [workflow management
  guide](https://www.3minread.com/master-workflow-management-definition-automation-and-best-practices-for-2025-success)
  explains how to map and prioritize work before any change lands.

  ## Verdict by Team Type


  These are our verdicts for four common profiles, based on the three criteria
  above.


  ### Product team of 4 people: choose Scrum


  Four people is small enough to share one Sprint Goal, and a two-week cadence
  gives stakeholders a predictable release date. Keep the roadmap stable and the
  Product Owner empowered. Switch to Kanban if your team keeps cancelling or
  rebuilding Sprints.


  ### Support team of 12 people: choose Kanban


  Support work arrives all day, priorities shift by the hour, and nothing is
  naturally sized for a two-week batch. Twelve people is above the usual Scrum
  range, so splitting one team would add complexity. Use Kanban with columns for
  triage, in progress, and waiting on customer, plus a work-in-progress limit
  per person. Track cycle time, because it shows how long customers actually
  wait.


  ### Marketing team of 2 people: choose Kanban


  Two people do not need prescribed roles or ceremonies. A simple board with
  columns for ideas, drafting, review, and published covers most work. If your
  campaigns run in fixed launch windows, a two-week Scrum cycle can work too,
  but Kanban keeps overhead lower.


  ### Development team of 8 with two-week releases: choose Scrum


  Eight developers in a cross-functional team with a regular release cycle is
  the classic Scrum profile. Sprint Planning and Review give the team a rhythm,
  and velocity helps you forecast without promising exact dates. If the team
  also handles a steady stream of bugs, add Kanban practices inside the Sprint.

  ## Moving From One Method to the Other


  You do not have to commit forever. Run a short pilot:


  1. Map the current work on one board, including every stage from request to
  done.

  2. Pick one team and run the new method for at least two Sprints or a
  comparable stretch of Kanban flow.

  3. Measure cycle time and Sprint Goal completion before and after.

  4. Decide, keep what works, and drop what does not.


  If you want a middle path, the Kanban Guide for Scrum Teams from
  [Scrum.org](https://www.scrum.org/scrum-kanban) describes how to use Kanban
  practices inside Scrum without replacing the Scrum Guide's rules. If you are
  still mapping your process, our guide to [workflow
  management](https://www.3minread.com/master-workflow-management-definition-automation-and-best-practices-for-2025-success)
  covers the basics before you pick a framework.


  ## Frequently Asked Questions


  ### Is Kanban better than Scrum for software teams?


  Neither is better in general. Scrum suits teams that want fixed cycles and
  shared goals. Kanban suits teams with continuous, interruption-heavy work. Use
  team size, delivery rhythm, and priority changes to decide.


  ### Can a team of three use Kanban?


  Yes. A three-person team can run Kanban with a simple board and a few
  work-in-progress limits. Small teams often find Kanban's light structure
  easier to maintain than Scrum's ceremonies.


  ### Does using Kanban mean dropping Sprints?


  Not necessarily. Kanban itself works in continuous flow and does not require
  Sprints. Some teams keep a regular review rhythm for planning and reflection,
  but that is a team choice rather than a Kanban rule.


  ### Can Kanban and Scrum be combined?


  Yes. Scrumban is a common hybrid. You can start by visualizing your workflow
  and limiting work in progress inside your existing Sprints, then adjust from
  there.


  ## Takeaway


  Pick Scrum if your team works in predictable cycles, shares a goal, and
  releases on a steady rhythm. Pick Kanban if work arrives continuously and
  priorities change often. Use team size as a rough filter, let delivery rhythm
  and priority changes make the final call, and run a short pilot before
  committing. The best workflow is the one your team actually follows.
