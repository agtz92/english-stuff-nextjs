mk1: >-
  Sales pipeline stages are the labeled checkpoints a prospect passes through on
  the way to a signed deal, and a small team usually needs five to seven of
  them. Each stage should have an exit criterion: a specific, checkable fact or
  action that must be true before a deal moves forward. Get those definitions
  right and your pipeline reports start to mean something. Skip them, and
  'qualified' can mean five different things to five different reps.


  Below, you will find a practical stage model, a way to write exit criteria
  that anyone on the team can check, and a sample tracking table you can copy
  into a basic CRM or spreadsheet.


  ## What are the typical sales pipeline stages?


  Most B2B pipelines follow the same arc, even when the names differ. This
  six-stage model suits a team with one to five sellers:


  1. **Lead.** A name enters your system through a form, referral, or outreach.
  *Exit:* you have confirmed a real person, a company, and a working way to
  reach them.

  2. **Qualified.** The prospect has a problem you can solve. *Exit:* the need,
  a rough budget range, and the decision-maker are all confirmed.

  3. **Discovery.** You run a call or demo to understand the problem in the
  buyer's own words. *Exit:* the buyer has agreed on what success looks like.

  4. **Proposal.** You send a written quote with scope, price, and timeline.
  *Exit:* the buyer has replied with questions or a decision date.

  5. **Negotiation.** You work through pricing, terms, and internal approvals.
  *Exit:* terms are agreed and a signature date is set.

  6. **Closed won or closed lost.** *Exit:* the contract is signed, or the loss
  reason is recorded.


  Post-sale work such as onboarding and renewals usually belongs to customer
  success, so keep it out of the pipeline. A deal leaves the pipeline when
  revenue is committed.
mk2: >-
  ## How many stages should a small-team pipeline have?


  A small-business playbook from [Confluunt
  Advisors](https://confluuntadvisors.com/insights/how-to-build-a-sales-pipeline-for-a-small-business-a-step-by-step-playbook)
  makes the case that five to seven stages is usually the most a small pipeline
  can carry before it becomes harder to maintain than the selling itself. Beyond
  that point, reps stop updating the pipeline, and your forecast turns into
  guesswork.


  Two simple tests help you decide where to cut. If two stages always get
  updated together, merge them. If a stage has no action a rep can take next, it
  is a status label, not a stage. A deal marked 'Interested' with nothing to do
  afterward belongs in Lead.


  ## How do you write exit criteria someone else can check?


  Entry criteria describe what must be true to enter a stage. Exit criteria
  describe what must be true to leave it. Most teams only write the exit side,
  and that is enough if the criteria are specific. [ClickYowl's guide to sales
  pipeline stages](https://clickyowl.com/sales-pipeline-stages/) frames exit
  criteria as the minimum facts or actions required before a deal advances.


  Good exit criteria are observable, dated, and tied to a named person. Weak
  ones are easy to satisfy and tell you little. The easiest way to see the
  difference is to describe the same deal two ways: seller-side wording records
  what the rep did, while buyer-side wording records what the customer did.
  Buyer actions are a better predictor of outcomes.

  ## How do you build a sample tracking table?


  You do not need an enterprise platform. Any CRM or spreadsheet with deal name,
  stage, value, owner, next step, due date, one yes/no field per exit check, and
  a win probability will work. The yes/no fields are the part most templates
  skip, and they keep reps from advancing deals on optimism alone.

  then recalibrating against your own closed deals. In this model, that means
  Lead at 10 percent, Qualified at 20, Discovery at 40, Proposal at 60, and
  Negotiation at 80. Treat these as a first draft and update them every quarter.

  ## How do you set a rule for stalled deals?


  Stalled deals are where small pipelines leak. Before you set a rule, make sure
  everyone agrees on [what counts as
  progress](#how-do-you-write-exit-criteria-someone-else-can-check). Then
  replace memory with a rule:


  - **No reply in 7 business days:** create a follow-up task for the owner.

  - **No activity in 21 days:** move the deal back one stage or flag it as
  stalled.

  - **No activity in 45 days:** review it, then either close it as lost with a
  reason or book a specific next step.


  These thresholds are a starting point, not an industry standard, so adjust
  them to your typical sales cycle. [HubSpot's pipeline
  guide](https://blog.hubspot.com/sales/sales-pipeline) describes a similar
  logic: automate a follow-up task when an opportunity goes quiet, then
  downgrade the stage if inactivity continues.


  ## What do the win-rate numbers tell you?


  Benchmarks are useful for context, but they vary widely. One 2025 analysis of
  Ebsta and Pavilion go-to-market data, summarized by
  [Pixelwand](https://www.pixelwand.io/blog/sales-pipeline-velocity-statistics),
  puts the average B2B win rate at about 18 percent, down from roughly 21
  percent a year earlier. The same analysis found that deals closing within 50
  days won around 47 percent of the time, compared with roughly 20 percent for
  deals that ran past that mark. Because these figures come from a single
  dataset, treat them as directional rather than fixed targets.


  A vendor-run study by
  [Optif](https://optif.ai/learn/questions/win-rate-by-sales-stage/) covering
  939 companies reports win rates that climb through the funnel: about 40
  percent at discovery, 55 percent at qualification, 70 percent at proposal, and
  85 percent at negotiation. Vendor studies carry their own incentives, so use
  the pattern as a reference point for your funnel rather than a target.


  On volume, [Saleshive's guide to pipeline
  management](https://saleshive.com/blog/sales-understanding-importance-pipeline-management)
  describes healthy teams as maintaining a coverage ratio of about 3:1 to 4:1,
  meaning three to four dollars of open pipeline for every dollar of quota.


  Your own numbers are the most useful benchmark. Track the percentage of deals
  that move from each stage to the next, and compare it with your last two
  quarters. That comparison will show you more than any published average.

  ## Which setup mistakes should you avoid?


  - **Too many stages.** Reps stop updating a long pipeline, and the data
  decays. Keep to the [stage model](#what-are-the-typical-sales-pipeline-stages)
  above.

  - **Activity names instead of buyer states.** 'Called the prospect' is an
  activity. 'Decision-maker confirmed' is a stage.

  - **No loss reasons.** Without a recorded reason, you cannot learn from lost
  deals.

  - **Probabilities that never change.** Recalibrate them against closed deals
  at least once a quarter.

  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### What are the stages of a sales pipeline?


  Most small teams use some version of lead, qualified, discovery, proposal,
  negotiation, and closed won or lost. Names vary, but each stage should
  describe a buyer state with a clear exit.


  ### How many stages should a sales pipeline have?


  Five to seven is a practical range for a small team. Fewer stages keep the
  pipeline current, while extra stages usually add detail that reps stop
  maintaining.


  ### What is the difference between entry and exit criteria?


  Entry criteria define what must be true to enter a stage. Exit criteria define
  what must be true to leave it. If you can enforce only one, start with exit
  criteria, because they control when a deal is allowed to advance.


  ### How do I track win rate by stage?


  Divide the number of deals that moved past a stage by the number that entered
  it, over a full sales cycle. Most CRMs can filter this with a stage report,
  and reviewing it monthly will show where deals stall.


  ## Key takeaway


  A good pipeline has a small number of stages, and each one has exit criteria a
  manager can verify in under a minute. Start with five to seven stages, write
  the exit criteria in buyer terms, add a yes/no check for each one, and set a
  rule for stalled deals. Review the numbers each quarter and adjust.
