  Winter solstice traditions around the world share one simple idea: the longest
  night of the year is a moment to gather, light something, and wait for the
  days to grow longer again. This quick tour covers three of the most
  distinctive customs, from Scandinavia's Yule to Iran's Yalda Night and Japan's
  Tōji, along with the historical reason behind each one. The solstice falls on
  December 21 or 22 in the Northern Hemisphere and in June in the Southern
  Hemisphere.


  At 3minread, we care less about listing customs and more about where they came
  from. So each section below separates what people do today from what the
  historical record supports.

  ## Japan: Tōji and the yuzu bath


  In Japan, the solstice is called Tōji. The best-known custom is the yuzu bath,
  a citrus-scented soak. The day also features kabocha squash, a winter staple
  that many families serve on the same day.


  ### Why yuzu goes in the bath


  Yuzu is a small, aromatic citrus. When it floats in hot water, its strong
  scent freshens the air. By association, people believed that scent drove away
  'bad air' and helped prevent colds. The idea is still part of the day's charm,
  and families often do it at home or visit a public bathhouse.


  ### Where the custom likely started


  The most-told origin story is less about health and more about business.
  According to [Nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-topics/b09723/), the
  practice is said to have begun as a way to attract customers when public
  bathhouses, or sento, were introduced in the Edo period (1603–1868). It's a
  good example of how a seasonal ritual can pick up a commercial reason along
  the way, while the cold-prevention belief stayed attached to it.

  ## Iran: Yalda Night and the longest night


  In Iran, the solstice is known as Yalda, or Shab-e Yalda. It is a family
  night. The household stays up together, often until dawn, which turns the
  longest night of the year into a shared event rather than a quiet one.


  ### A night-long vigil with poetry and fruit


  Families traditionally gather inside the home around the korsi, a low table
  with a heat source underneath and blankets draped over it, while bonfires are
  lit outside. Reading poetry is a central part of the evening, and verses are
  often shared aloud. Pomegranates and watermelon are the best-known fruits on
  the spread, along with nuts and dried fruit. Their bright colors make the
  table feel like a small celebration of light in the dark.


  ### Where the name and the age come from


  Yalda's roots are usually traced to Zoroastrian tradition, in which the
  darkest, longest night of the year was seen as an unfavorable time, so people
  gathered together to get through it. Accounts of its age vary widely. ICRO's
  Yalda overview-) places the night's official entry into the ancient Iranian
  calendar in 502 BC, under Darius I. Popular accounts online often push its
  origins back more than 7,000 years. The useful takeaway is that the custom is
  very old, and its exact starting point depends on which source you read.


  ### Why UNESCO recognition matters


  In 2022, UNESCO added Yalda Night to its Representative List of the Intangible
  Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The listing recognizes a living practice, not
  only a historical one. Iranian media have since marked the anniversary, and
  Tehran Times reported on the third anniversary of the designation.

  ## Scandinavia: Yule, Lucia, and the straw goat


  Scandinavia is where solstice traditions get most tangled up with later
  customs. Yule is the best example. Today it is simply the everyday word for
  Christmas in several languages, but its older meaning was a midwinter feast
  held on a calendar that was not the same as ours.


  ### Yule was not always on the solstice


  The earliest written record points to midwinter timing. In 725, the
  Anglo-Saxon scholar Bede recorded two months called Giuli, one on either side
  of the winter solstice. Historians generally place the pre-Christian Yule
  feast later in midwinter rather than on the solstice itself. [Mythic
  Harmonies' guide to the Norse midwinter
  feast](https://www.mythic-harmonies.com/viking-culture/yule-norse-midwinter-feast/)
  walks through that distinction. Yule was also a multi-night celebration, which
  is a useful reminder that it was a season, not a single evening.


  ### Customs that came later


  Many customs now sold as ancient Viking traditions, including the Yule log and
  the straw goat, appear in written sources centuries later. That doesn't make
  them less meaningful. It means they are best understood as folk customs that
  developed over time, rather than as proof of exactly how the Vikings
  celebrated.


  ### Why Lucia falls on December 13


  Sweden's St. Lucia's Day falls on December 13, with processions led by a girl
  wearing a crown of candles. The date is the clue to the calendar. Her saint
  day was December 13, which in the old Julian calendar was the winter solstice,
  as [Seattle Sami's essay on the ancient origins of
  Yule](https://seattlesami.org/2012/12/04/ancient-origins-of-yule/) explains.
  The Julian calendar ran slightly longer than the solar year, so its dates
  slowly drifted away from the sun. The 1582 Gregorian reform corrected that
  drift, but the saint's date stayed on December 13. Today Lucia falls about a
  week before the solstice.


  ## Why the longest night matters


  The solstice happens because of Earth's tilt. Around December 21 or 22 in the
  Northern Hemisphere, the tilted axis points farthest from the sun, so the day
  is shortest and the night is longest. After that point, daylight returns a
  little more each day. That slow return is why so many cultures chose this
  moment for lights, feasts, and stories.

  ## Ways to mark the solstice at home


  If you want to borrow a few ideas from these traditions, keep it simple:


  - Dim the lights after dark and light a single candle, since both Lucia
  processions and Yalda vigils center on light.

  - Share a bowl of pomegranate, citrus, or nuts, and talk about what you hope
  to see in the coming months.

  - Read a short poem aloud, a nod to the Yalda habit of sharing verse together.

  - Treat it as a night, not an evening. Stay in and keep the pace slow.


  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### When is the winter solstice?


  In the Northern Hemisphere, the winter solstice falls on December 21 or 22. In
  the Southern Hemisphere, it falls in June, usually on June 20 or 21.


  ### Is Yule the same as Christmas?


  Not exactly. Yule was a pre-Christian midwinter feast. After Scandinavia
  converted to Christianity, its name became the everyday word for Christmas in
  Scandinavian languages.


  ### What do Iranians eat on Yalda night?


  The classic choices are pomegranates, watermelon, nuts, and dried fruit.
  Families share them through the night while poetry is read.


  ### Why do people take a yuzu bath on Tōji?


  The bath is tied to the belief that yuzu's scent freshens the air and helps
  prevent colds. That belief has stayed with the custom, and its origin story
  also links it to the bathhouses of the Edo period.


  ## The takeaway


  Winter solstice traditions around the world have different roots, but they
  share a clear purpose: marking the longest night and the return of light.
  Japan's yuzu bath, Iran's Yalda vigil, and Scandinavia's Yule and Lucia
  traditions each show how a seasonal date can collect new meanings over
