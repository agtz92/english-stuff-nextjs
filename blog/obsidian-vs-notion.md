  ## Where do your notes actually live?


  Obsidian stores everything you write as plain markdown files on your device.
  If you stop using Obsidian, those files still open in any text editor. That is
  the strongest case for the app, and a real comfort for anyone worried about
  lock-in. Notion works the other way. Pages and databases live in its cloud,
  which makes syncing across devices and sharing a page effortless.


  The trade-off is responsibility. With Obsidian, backups and sync are your job
  unless you pay for Obsidian Sync or keep your vault in a folder you already
  sync. With Notion, you get convenience but depend on its servers and its
  pricing. A local vault is only as safe as your backup habit, while a cloud
  workspace is only as portable as its export options.

  ## Which one handles databases better?


  This is where the answer depends on how structured your information is. Notion
  databases are native: you add a Status select, a Due date, and a relation to
  another database, then switch between table, board, and calendar views.
  Obsidian takes a different route. Each note carries properties, and the
  built-in Bases feature turns those properties into table-like views you can
  filter and sort. It's flexible, but it feels like assembling parts rather than
  using a finished product.


  Take a reading list with a status, a due date, and a link to each author.
  Here's how it looks in each app.


  ### Reading list in Notion


  - Create a Books database with Status (To read, Reading, Done) and Due date
  properties.

  - Add an Author relation to a People database. The links show up on both sides
  automatically.

  - Build a board view grouped by Status for a quick overview.


  ### Reading list in Obsidian


  - Create one note per book with status, due, and author properties at the top.

  - Link each author name, so every author note shows its backlinks.

  - Build a Bases view filtered on status to see what you're reading right now.


  The workaround to know: Obsidian's links give you backlinks, but you won't get
  Notion-style rollups or relation fields that update inside a single table. If
  your project needs totals across related records, Notion is the safer pick.
  Check Obsidian's current Bases documentation before you commit, because the
  feature keeps changing.


  ## Can you really collaborate in Obsidian?


  Notion is built for shared work. Several people can edit the same page,
  comment, and manage permissions inside the workspace. Obsidian was built for
  personal knowledge. Sharing a vault with a small team runs through Obsidian
  Sync, which merges edits when it syncs. There are no live cursors like the
  ones Notion shows, so two people editing the same note at the same moment can
  create a messy merge.


  For a small team of two to five people, here's a workflow that works in each
  tool:


  - Obsidian: one shared vault, one owner per folder, and a rule that each
  person edits their own notes and discusses changes elsewhere. Each member
  needs their own Sync seat.

  - Notion: one teamspace, a page owner for each project, and comments for
  discussion. Pages update live for everyone.

  The counterargument for Notion is cost. Its convenience grows with every
  member on a paid plan, so the team has to decide whether live editing is worth
  the monthly line item.

  ## How hard is the learning curve?


  Notion is easier to start. You type, press slash, and get a table, to-do list,
  or template in seconds. Obsidian asks more of you early on. You learn link
  syntax, how folders and links work together, and which plugins to trust. In
  our view, Obsidian is harder to start but easier to outgrow, because your
  notes stay portable and you add structure only when you need it.


  You can test this yourself. Set a timer and build the same 20-book reading
  list in each app, with three properties and one link per book. Note how long
  it takes and how often you stop to search for help. Your own number will tell
  you more than any feature table.

  ## Can you move between them, and what breaks?


  Moving from Notion to Obsidian is common, and Obsidian's importer can bring
  over Notion pages and databases. The open question is what survives. Database
  properties, relations, and views don't always arrive in the same form, so test
  with one database before you migrate everything. Export it, import it, and
  list which properties, relations, and views came through intact. Going the
  other way, markdown notes move into Notion as pages, but you'll likely rebuild
  links and any Bases views by hand.


  ## Which one works offline?


  Obsidian works offline by default because your vault sits on your device.
  Notion's help center says all workspace members on all plans can use pages
  offline, and offline pages are kept only on that device, per [Notion's guide
  to using pages offline](https://www.notion.com/help/use-pages-offline).
  Offline mode works in the desktop and mobile apps. If you write on planes,
  trains, or unreliable Wi-Fi, Obsidian is the simpler promise. Notion works
  offline for the pages you've prepared in advance.

  - Solo writer or researcher: choose Obsidian. Local files, linking, and
  offline access suit long-term personal knowledge. The strongest
  counterargument is that you own the system, which means you maintain it.

  - Developer: choose Obsidian if you like plain text, your own editor, and
  version-controlled notes. Choose Notion if your project boards need teammates.

  - Student: choose Obsidian for notes you'll keep for years. Choose Notion if
  your study group shares a database and you want a fast start.

  - Small team: choose Notion. Obsidian works for two people who take turns, but
  live co-editing is where it struggles.

  - Non-technical team: choose Notion. The learning curve is low, and shared
  pages are the core product. The risk is cost, which grows with each member and
  with plan choice.


  ### If you start more projects than you finish


  If your problem isn't where your notes live but that you drop projects and
  can't remember where you stopped, a notes app may not be the fix.
  [continuu.it](https://continuu.it) is a project tool built around that
  problem. Every project has a state (Active, Paused, Completed, or Killed), so
  a stalled project is labeled as stalled. Tasks live inside each project, and
  notes keep the context you'd otherwise have to rebuild after three weeks away.
  Ideas can be parked without becoming projects, killed projects go to a
  Graveyard, and analytics show how much you start against how much you finish.
  It also covers recurring routines and a calendar, and a free plan is
  available. It isn't a replacement for Notion as a docs or wiki tool, so if you
  need a shared wiki, keep Notion or Obsidian for that and use continuu.it for
  the projects themselves.

  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### Is Obsidian free?


  Yes for the core app, which is free for personal and commercial use. Sync and
  Publish are optional paid add-ons.


  ### Is Notion free?


  Yes, there is a free plan with limits, including 5 MB file uploads, 7 days of
  page history, and 10 external guests. Paid plans are billed per member.


  ### Can I use Obsidian and Notion together?


  Yes. A common split is personal notes in Obsidian and shared project pages in
  Notion, with only the shared material copied across.


  ### Does Notion work offline?


  Yes, for pages you've made available offline in the desktop and mobile apps.
  Offline pages stay on the device where you saved them.


  ### Is Obsidian good for teams?


  For two people who take turns editing, Obsidian Sync can work. For live
  co-editing, Notion is the better fit.


  ## The takeaway


  Choose Obsidian for local-first, personal notes you own and can take anywhere.
