  Keyboard layouts explained in one sentence: the size name tells you roughly
  how a board compares with a standard full-size keyboard, and which keys it
  leaves out. A 60%, 65%, 75%, TKL, or full-size board each trades keys for desk
  space in a different way, so the right pick depends on how you type and how
  much room you have.


  If you enter long strings of numbers every day, full-size is the answer. If
  you want arrows and the function row but no numpad, TKL is the easiest choice.
  If your desk is tight, a 75% or 65% board recovers space while keeping most of
  the keys you actually use.


  ## What the percentages in keyboard names mean


  The percentage is shorthand, not a measurement. It compares a board's key
  count with a standard 104-key US full-size keyboard, the baseline most names
  are built on. A 60% board has about 60 keys, and a 75% board has around 81 to
  84.


  Two things follow from that. First, the name tells you what is missing more
  reliably than it tells you the exact width. Second, boards with the same name
  can differ by a few millimeters in layout and case size, so the spec sheet is
  the final check before you buy.

  ## Full-size: every key, at the cost of desk space


  A full-size board has the main block, a function row, a navigation cluster,
  arrow keys, and a numpad on the right. The US version has 104 keys. The ISO
  version adds one key beside the left Shift and uses a taller, L-shaped Enter
  key. Both follow the layout principles set out in the ISO/IEC 9995 standard
  series for keyboard layouts for text and office systems.


  Full-size is the right pick when:


  - You enter numbers all day, for spreadsheets, invoices, or data entry.

  - Your desk is wide enough that the numpad doesn't block your mouse path.

  - You use F-keys often and don't want to learn a layer system.


  The trade-off is width. At roughly 440 to 450 mm, a full-size board pushes
  your mouse hand far to the right, which is the main reason people look at
  smaller formats.

  ## TKL: the everyday middle ground


  A tenkeyless (TKL) board removes only the numpad. You keep the function row,
  the navigation block, and the arrow keys in their familiar places. That makes
  TKL the most common choice for people who want most of the full-size layout
  without the extra width. At about 355 mm wide, it is roughly 90 mm narrower
  than a typical full-size board.


  It's a strong default for gaming, coding, and general office work. Our view:
  if you're unsure, start here. You give up a numpad most people rarely use, and
  you keep the keys you reach for without thinking.


  ### Why the names don't line up


  Naming history is messy. [Deskthority's community
  wiki](https://deskthority.net/wiki/Tenkeyless_keyboard) describes 'tenkeyless'
  as a term that has also been used for what are now called 75% boards, and some
  sources call that size 85%. TKL is also sometimes called an 80% board, because
  around 87 keys is close to 80% of a 104-key full-size layout.


  Don't rely on the label alone. Check the key count and the layout diagram
  before you buy.


  ## 75%: same core keys, a different hand path


  The 75% layout keeps the function row and compresses the navigation keys into
  a narrow column on the right. The case is about 320 mm wide. The bigger change
  is for your hands. On most 75% boards, the arrow keys move away from the
  classic cluster and sit under the navigation column.


  What that means in practice:


  - Your right hand has to find the arrows in a new spot.

  - Home, End, Page Up, and Page Down sit in a short column, so you find them by
  position rather than by a separate block.

  - Muscle memory built on a full-size or TKL board needs some adjustment time.


  If you move through code or documents with the arrow keys without looking,
  test a 75% before you commit.

  ## 65% and 60%: smaller footprints, more layers


  A 65% board drops the function row and the numpad, but it keeps the arrow keys
  and a navigation column. For many desks, it's the most practical compromise.
  At about 315 mm wide, it is only slightly narrower than a 75%.


  A 60% board is the smallest common size. It removes the function row, the
  navigation keys, and the dedicated arrow keys, so you reach those through a Fn
  layer. Its case is about 30 cm wide, which in the same retailer comparison
  works out to roughly 14 cm narrower than a full-size board. That saving is why
  people choose it for tight desks and travel.


  Our view: a 60% suits people who already like layers and want the smallest
  footprint. For a first mechanical board, a TKL or 65% usually has a shorter
  learning curve.


  ## Which layout fits your desk


  Before you buy, measure your desk and run through this checklist:


  1. Measure the width and depth of the usable area, not the whole desk.

  2. Mark where your mouse sits and leave room for its path and pad.

  3. Check the numpad side. If a wall, monitor arm, or speaker blocks the right
  edge, a full-size board creates a reach problem.

  4. Leave depth for a wrist rest and for the cable.

  5. List the keys you use every day. If you never touch the numpad, you're
  paying for width you won't use.


  Then match the layout to your setup:


  - **Small desk:** 60% or 65%. They free the most width and keep the mouse
  closer to the center.

  - **Corner desk:** TKL or 75%. Without a numpad, the right side stays clear of
  the wall.

  - **Shared desk:** A compact board leaves room for someone else's gear. Choose
  full-size only if you need numbers all day.

  - **Standing desk:** TKL or 65%. A narrower board keeps the mouse closer to
  your body.

  - **Laptop plus external keyboard:** TKL is a common match for laptop users
  who want arrows and F-keys. Choose full-size only if spreadsheets are a daily
  task.

  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### What is a 75% keyboard?


  A 75% keyboard is a compact board with about 81 to 84 keys. It drops the
  numpad, keeps a function row, and compresses the navigation keys into a
  column. You get a smaller footprint than a TKL without giving up F-keys.


  ### Is TKL or 75% better for programming?


  TKL is the lower-friction choice if you navigate code with arrow keys in their
  usual place. A 75% works well if you want the function row in a smaller
  footprint and don't mind a different arrow position. Our view: start with TKL
  unless your desk is tight.


  ### Is a 60% keyboard good for beginners?


  Usually not as a first board. A 60% relies on a Fn layer for arrows and
  navigation, which takes time to learn. A TKL or 65% is an easier start, and
  you can move to a 60% later.


  ### Do I need a numpad if I type numbers often?


  Yes, if numbers are a daily task such as data entry or accounting. A full-size
  board keeps the numpad on the right. If you enter numbers only occasionally, a
  TKL with the standard number row is usually enough.


  ### Is 65% a good middle ground?


  For many desks, yes. It keeps the arrow keys and drops the function row and
  numpad, which suits people who want more room without learning a heavy layer
  system. It's a judgment call, so test the layout if you can.


  ## The bottom line


  Keyboard layouts explained in short: decide which keys you use every day, then
  check how much desk you have. Full-size keeps every key. TKL removes only the
  numpad and keeps the arrows and F-keys where you expect them. 75% and 65% trim
  more width, and 60% gives the smallest footprint in exchange for learning
  layers. Measure first, match the layout to your desk, and test the arrow
  placement before you commit.
