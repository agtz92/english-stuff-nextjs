  How to set boundaries when working from home comes down to three things: fixed
  working hours, a clear signal that the workday is over, and notification
  settings that match both. Pick a start and stop time, choose one place where
  work happens, and decide in advance which messages can reach you after hours.
  The checklist below gives you rules you can apply today, plus a sample
  shutdown routine you can copy and adjust.


  ## Why boundaries matter more at home


  When the office is a few steps from the kitchen, the workday rarely ends on
  its own. Email sits on your phone, and the laptop is always within reach. The
  problem is not only discipline. It is recovery. A 2016 meta-analysis in
  [Frontiers in
  Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02072/full)
  found that psychological detachment from work correlates positively with
  self-reported mental and physical health and well-being across the studies it
  reviewed.


  A 2025 [Springer study on psychological
  detachment](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-025-00883-7) went
  further. It linked each one-point increase in detachment to higher
  satisfaction with health, family life, and sleep, and sleep satisfaction rose
  by 0.31 points. Detachment is the skill of mentally leaving work, and a
  boundary is the tool that makes it possible.


  The pressure is common. In a 2022 survey, [The Conference
  Board](https://www.conference-board.org/press/Remote-Workers-Struggle)
  reported that nearly half of remote workers (47 percent) were concerned about
  blurred work-life boundaries, including trouble unplugging and feeling always
  on. If that sounds familiar, you are not the only one.

  ## A checklist for setting work-from-home boundaries


  ### 1. Set fixed hours and say them out loud


  - Choose a start time and a stop time you can keep most days.

  - Block them in your calendar as a recurring event.

  - Show them in your chat status or email signature, so people know when you
  are available.

  - Tell your manager in writing. A short message works: 'I'm working 9:00 to
  5:30 Eastern. I'm available for calls in that window, and I'll answer
  non-urgent messages the next working day.'


  ### 2. Give work a place, even a small one


  - A room with a door is ideal, but a desk, a corner, or one specific chair can
  do the job.

  - Avoid working in bed or on the couch when you can. Those spots tell your
  brain it's time to rest.

  - At the end of the day, physically change the space. Close the laptop, move
  the chair, or put your work items in a bag.


  ### 3. Set notification rules before you need them


  - Turn off non-essential alerts on work devices, including social media and
  shopping apps.

  - Use a scheduled Do Not Disturb or quiet hours setting in your phone and your
  chat app. A schedule keeps working when you forget to mute by hand.

  - If your team runs on a shared collaboration platform, see our guide to [the
  top sales collaboration tools of
  2025](https://www.3minread.com/the-top-sales-collaboration-tools-of-2025-that-are-actually-worth-your-teams-time)
  for options worth a look.

  - Keep work apps off your personal phone after hours if you can. If you can't,
  log out of the work account or use a separate work profile.


  ### 4. Define 'urgent' in advance


  Most after-hours messages are not emergencies. Agree on a one-line definition
  with your team, such as: 'Urgent means a client system is down or a deadline
  is missed today.' Then name one real channel for urgent issues, such as a
  phone call or a specific text, so people have a route for true emergencies and
  a clear reason not to use it for everything else.


  ### 5. Close the day with a ritual


  A ritual gives your brain a clear end point. The example below takes about 15
  minutes.

  ### A template for after-hours replies


  Use this for non-urgent messages that arrive after your stop time: 'Thanks for
  reaching out. I'm offline for the day and will look at this first thing on my
  next working day. If it's urgent, please call me at [number] or use
  [channel].'


  ## Where unfinished work goes at the end of the day


  For many people, the hardest part of logging off is not the work itself. It's
  the list of half-finished projects, open questions, and ideas that keeps
  running in the background. Writing those items down somewhere outside your
  head can help you stop carrying them home.


  One option is [continuu.it](https://continuu.it), a project tool for people
  who start more than they finish. Every project has a state (Active, Paused,
  Killed, or Completed), so paused work is clearly paused instead of quietly
  pending. Tasks live inside projects, notes keep the context you would
  otherwise have to rebuild, and ideas can be parked without becoming projects
  yet. Projects you decide to drop go to a Graveyard, because killing a project
  counts as finishing it. The tool also handles recurring routines and has a
  calendar of real dates. A free plan is available.


  It's built for freelancers, students with long projects, and side-project
  builders, so it may be more than a standard remote job needs. If your team
  needs a broader all-in-one platform,
  [monday.com](https://www.3minread.com/what-is-monday-com-why-everyone-from-startups-to-enterprises-is-using-this-all-in-one-work-os)
  is another option to compare. In the shutdown routine above, a tool like this
  fits the 5:05 step: note where you stopped and which projects are active or
  paused, so tomorrow you aren't rebuilding context from memory.

  ## What doesn't work for everyone


  The checklist assumes you have a spare room, a flexible schedule, and a
  household that respects your hours. Many people don't have all three. Here is
  how to adapt.


  ### If you don't have a spare room


  A separate room helps, but it isn't required. A designated desk, a corner, or
  a specific chair can signal when it's time to focus and when it's time to step
  away. If your desk is also the dining table, use a physical reset: clear it
  every evening and put the laptop away in a bag. If the workspace is always in
  view, the visual cue may feel weak. In that case, lean on time-based rules,
  such as fixed hours and a notification schedule, which do most of the work.


  ### If you share the space or the hours


  Roommates, partners, and kids need to know your schedule, but they won't guess
  it. Agree on a 'do not interrupt' signal, such as a closed door, headphones,
  or a sign on the desk. Expect the first two weeks to be messy. If another
  adult in the home works on a different schedule, set shared quiet hours
  instead of making one-off requests every day.


  ### If you're caregiving


  Caregiving schedules rarely fit a neat 9-to-5. Instead of fixed blocks, try
  task blocks: two or three focused windows that fit around care, plus a short
  list of tasks that can be done in pieces. Tell your manager which windows you
  can cover and when you may need flexibility. Don't promise a stop time you
  can't keep. A realistic rule you follow beats a perfect one you abandon.


  ### If your team spans time zones


  When your team works in another time zone, your end of day may overlap with
  someone else's start. Pick a shared overlap window of a few hours for live
  work and treat the rest as asynchronous. Write down which requests can wait
  for the other side's morning, and say so in your status message. That way,
  nobody has to guess whether a late reply is a problem.

  ## Questions people ask


  ### How do I stop working when my desk is in my bedroom?


  Move the work out of sight at the end of the day. Close the laptop, put it in
  a bag or drawer, and cover the desk if you can. Pair that with a fixed stop
  time and a short transition activity, such as a walk around the block. The
  physical change matters because your brain links the room with the task.


  ### Should I turn off Slack notifications after work?


  For most people, yes, at least for non-urgent channels. Use a notification
  schedule instead of muting by hand, and keep one urgent channel that can still
  reach you. That way, you don't miss a real emergency, and you don't spend the
  evening answering routine questions.


  ### Is it normal to work longer hours at home?


  It's common. The commute disappears and the laptop is always nearby, so hours
  creep up without anyone noticing. If your day runs long for more than a few
  weeks, review your workload with your manager rather than simply working
  faster to keep up.


  ### How do I say no to extra work when I work from home?


  Name the trade-off instead of refusing outright. Try: 'I can take this on if
  we move the report to next week. Which one is the priority?' That puts the
  decision with the person assigning the work and makes the cost of extra tasks
  visible.


  ### Do right-to-disconnect laws apply to remote workers?


  Some do, depending on where you live. France has recognized a right to
  disconnect since 2017. Rules differ by country and, in the United States, by
  state, and some of these laws focus on employer obligations rather than
  individual rights. If your job crosses borders, check your local rules or ask
  HR before assuming you're protected.


  ## The takeaway


  Learning how to set boundaries when working from home starts with fixed hours,
  one workspace cue, a notification schedule, a clear definition of urgent, and
  a shutdown ritual. Test the system for two weeks, then keep what you actually
  follow and drop what doesn't fit your home. A boundary that works on most days
  is more useful than a perfect one you can't maintain.
