  If you are searching for KPI examples for small business, here is the short
  answer: track a small set of numbers that show cash, customers, and growth,
  and review them on a fixed schedule. A key performance indicator (KPI) is a
  metric you act on. If a number could double tomorrow and you would change
  nothing, it is a metric, not a KPI.


  Below are ten KPIs you can calculate in a spreadsheet. Each one comes with a
  plain formula, a worked example, and the limitations that many lists leave
  out. All examples use Juniper Candle Co., a fictional online shop run by one
  owner with two part-time packers. In one month, it had $20,000 in revenue from
  400 orders, $8,000 in cost of goods sold, $9,000 in operating expenses
  (including $3,000 in marketing), and 120 new customers. Of its marketing
  budget, $1,000 went to paid ads that generated $5,000 in tracked sales. Using
  one business keeps the numbers consistent across every metric.


  ## Ten KPI Examples for Small Business, With Formulas

  ## Financial KPIs


  ### 1. Gross Margin


  **Formula:** (Revenue - Cost of goods sold) ÷ Revenue × 100


  **Example:** Juniper's gross profit is $20,000 - $8,000 = $12,000. Gross
  margin is $12,000 ÷ $20,000 = 60%. Of every dollar in sales, 60 cents is left
  after product costs.


  **Limitation:** Gross profit comes before overhead, so a healthy margin does
  not prove the business is profitable. As
  [Bluevine](https://www.bluevine.com/blog/financial-kpis) notes, gross profit
  is calculated before other expenses are subtracted. The number is also only as
  good as your cost data. If wax, wicks, jars, and shipping supplies are missing
  from cost of goods sold, the margin looks better than it is. There is no
  universal target, so compare your margin with your own history and pricing.


  ### 2. Net Profit Margin


  **Formula:** Net profit ÷ Revenue × 100


  **Example:** Juniper's operating profit is $12,000 gross profit minus $9,000
  operating expenses, which equals $3,000. Assuming no interest or taxes that
  month, net profit is also $3,000, so the net profit margin is $3,000 ÷ $20,000
  = 15%.


  **Limitation:** One month can swing because of a one-time repair, a large
  equipment purchase, or owner pay that is not yet recorded. Taxes and loan
  interest can shrink the final figure, so the result is only as complete as
  your bookkeeping. A 15% margin is a result, not a benchmark, and it says
  little about whether the business can fund growth.


  ### 3. Operating Expense Ratio


  **Formula:** Operating expenses ÷ Revenue × 100


  **Example:** $9,000 ÷ $20,000 = 45%. For every dollar Juniper sells, 40 cents
  goes to product cost and 45 cents goes to overhead, which leaves 15 cents of
  profit.


  **Limitation:** The ratio can look better when revenue rises, even if overhead
  grows in dollar terms. Track it alongside the dollar totals. Accountants also
  classify some costs differently, such as whether software is an operating
  expense or part of cost of goods sold. Pick one rule and apply it every month
  so the trend stays meaningful.


  ### 4. Cash Runway


  **Formula:** Cash on hand ÷ Average monthly net cash burn


  **Example:** Juniper is profitable this month, so it has no burn to measure.
  Consider a slow quarter in which cash outflows exceed inflows by $6,000 a
  month and the bank balance is $36,000. Cash runway is $36,000 ÷ $6,000 = 6
  months.


  **Limitation:** The formula assumes the burn rate stays flat. It ignores
  seasonal sales, bills due in the next few weeks, loan repayments, and tax
  payments. Profitable businesses can still run short of cash because of timing,
  so check cash flow directly as well as runway. This figure works best as a
  weekly check, because cash moves faster than monthly reports.


  ### 5. Revenue Growth Rate


  **Formula:** (Current period revenue - Prior period revenue) ÷ Prior period
  revenue × 100


  **Example:** Juniper's revenue was $16,000 last month and $20,000 this month.
  Growth is ($20,000 - $16,000) ÷ $16,000 = 25% month over month.


  **Limitation:** Month-over-month growth misleads in seasonal businesses.
  Candle sales often peak around the holidays, so compare the same month in the
  previous year when you judge performance. Growth bought with heavy discounts
  can also hide weak margins, so read this KPI next to gross margin.

  ## Customer and Marketing KPIs


  ### 6. Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)


  **Formula:** Total acquisition spending ÷ New customers acquired


  **Example:** Juniper spent $3,000 on marketing and gained 120 new customers.
  CAC is $3,000 ÷ 120 = $25 per customer.


  **Limitation:** Marketing-only CAC excludes costs that also bring in
  customers, such as sales salaries, sample products, and discounts used to
  close deals. Timing matters too: spending in March may produce customers in
  April, so a single month can be distorted. Averaging over three months gives a
  steadier number. Define what counts as acquisition spending before you compare
  results over time.


  ### 7. Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)


  **Formula:** Average order value × Orders per year × Years a customer stays ×
  Gross margin %


  **Example:** Juniper's average order is $50, customers order about three times
  a year, and they stay about two years. Revenue CLV is $50 × 3 × 2 = $300. At a
  60% gross margin, gross-margin CLV is $180. Compared with the $25 CAC, that is
  $180 ÷ $25 = 7.2. Some small businesses use a 3:1 ratio as a rough check.
  Treat that as a rule of thumb, not a standard. For a step-by-step walkthrough,
  read our [customer lifetime value
  explainer](https://www.3minread.com/customer-lifetime-value-explained).


  **Limitation:** CLV depends on estimates of how long customers stay and how
  often they buy. Until you have two years of data, the two-year figure is an
  assumption. Newer customers often behave differently from early adopters, and
  the formula does not discount future profit to today's dollars.


  ### 8. Customer Churn Rate


  **Formula:** Customers lost during the period ÷ Customers at the start of the
  period × 100


  This is the definition used in Ninety's list of small business KPIs.


  **Example:** Juniper started the month with 200 repeat customers and lost 10
  of them. Churn is 10 ÷ 200 × 100 = 5% for the month.


  **Limitation:** Lost is hard to define for products people buy once or twice a
  year. A customer who has not reordered may still be happy. Monthly and annual
  churn are not interchangeable, so 5% a month is a very different situation
  from 5% a year. Confirm the time window before you compare numbers.


  ### 9. Average Order Value (AOV)


  **Formula:** Total revenue ÷ Number of orders


  **Example:** $20,000 ÷ 400 orders = $50 per order.


  **Limitation:** A few large orders, such as a corporate gift purchase, can
  inflate the average. A bundle discount can lower AOV while still raising total
  profit if more people buy. Check the median order value next to the average so
  one unusual sale does not mislead you.


  ### 10. Return on Ad Spend (ROAS)


  **Formula:** Revenue from ads ÷ Ad spend × 100


  **Example:** Juniper's $1,000 in paid ads generated $5,000 in tracked sales.
  ROAS is $5,000 ÷ $1,000 = 500%, or a 5:1 ratio. Revenue is not profit, though.
  At a 60% gross margin, those $5,000 in sales produce $3,000 in gross profit,
  so the campaign contributed $2,000 after the ad cost.


  **Limitation:** Attribution is imperfect. Ad platforms may claim sales that
  would have happened anyway, and they can miss customers who saw an ad and
  bought later through another channel. Use one attribution window consistently
  so you compare like with like.

  ## Which KPIs Fit a Solo Business?


  The U.S. Small Business Administration's [2024 advocacy
  release](https://advocacy.sba.gov/2024/11/19/new-advocacy-report-shows-small-business-total-reaches-34-8-million-accounting-for-2-6-million-net-new-jobs-in-latest-year-of-data/)
  counted 34.8 million small businesses. Many of them have no employees, which
  changes which metrics matter. Staff-based measures such as revenue per
  employee tell a one-person business very little.


  For a solo operator, the most useful set is usually gross margin, cash runway,
  CAC, and churn. These work at any size. If you sell physical stock, add
  inventory turnover, calculated as cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory.
  [Xero's KPI guide](https://www.xero.com/us/guides/kpis-for-business/) explains
  the metric and how to read it.


  ## Leading vs. Lagging KPIs


  Lagging KPIs report what has already happened, such as revenue and net profit.
  Leading KPIs signal what is likely to happen next, such as qualified leads,
  wholesale inquiries, or repeat visits to your site. Leading indicators give
  you time to act. If Juniper's wholesale inquiries fall for two months in a
  row, revenue often follows a few weeks later, and the owner has time to
  adjust. Our [sales forecasting guide for small
  teams](https://www.3minread.com/sales-forecasting-small-teams) shows how to
  turn these signals into a simple forecast.


  ## How Many KPIs Should You Track?


  Sources disagree. Some suggest three to five, while others say six to eight is
  enough. Start with three to five, one for each area that matters most: cash,
  profit, and customers. Each KPI should have an owner, a target or acceptable
  range, a review date, and a clear action.


  ## Decide the Action Before You Measure


  A KPI earns its place when it changes what you do. Write the trigger down in
  advance. For example:


  - If gross margin falls below your pricing floor, review supplier costs before
  raising your ad budget.

  - If CAC rises above one-third of CLV, pause the weakest campaign and check
  the numbers. This is a rule of thumb, not a standard.

  - If churn rises two months in a row, contact lapsed customers and ask why
  they stopped buying.

  - If cash runway drops below three months, delay non-essential spending.

  ## Frequently Asked Questions


  ### What is the difference between a metric and a KPI?


  A metric is any number you can measure. A KPI is a metric tied to a goal, with
  a target and an action you will take if it moves. Revenue is a metric. Revenue
  growth measured against a quarterly target is a KPI.


  ### How many KPIs should a small business track?


  Most small businesses do well with three to five. Start with cash, profit, and
  customers, then add more only when you have a clear reason to watch another
  number.


  ### What is the difference between leading and lagging KPIs?


  Lagging KPIs measure results, such as revenue or net profit. Leading KPIs
  measure early signals, such as sales leads or repeat site visits, that point
  to future results. Use both: lagging metrics confirm what happened, and
  leading metrics give you time to respond.


  ### Is revenue per employee useful for a one-person business?


  Usually not. If the owner is the only person counted, the metric just repeats
  total revenue, so it adds no insight. Focus on margin, cash, customer
  acquisition, and retention instead.


  ### How often should KPIs be reviewed?


  Review most KPIs monthly and your cash position weekly. Monthly reviews give
  enough data to see trends, while weekly cash checks catch timing problems
  early.


  ## Conclusion


  KPIs work when there are few of them, each one is clearly defined, and each is
  tied to a decision. Start with a handful of the ten above, calculate them the
  same way every month, and note the limitations next to each number. That habit
  is the difference between collecting numbers and managing a business. It is
  also the approach we use at 3minread when we explain business metrics to
  readers who are new to them.
