  Bourbon vs rye whiskey is mostly a question of grain. Bourbon must be made
  from a fermented mash of at least 51% corn, and rye must be made from at least
  51% rye. Everything else you taste, from caramel sweetness to peppery bite,
  comes from the rest of the recipe, the barrel, and the way the spirit was
  made.


  Here is the short answer: bourbon usually leans sweet and rounded, while rye
  usually leans dry and spicy. The label alone won't tell you much, though,
  because both categories cover a wide range. In this guide we start with the
  legal rules, then look at the mash bill spectrum that most comparisons skip,
  and finish with named bottles and a simple tasting you can run at home.


  ## Bourbon vs Rye Whiskey: What the Law Requires


  The U.S. standards for whiskey are set in federal regulation, found in [27 CFR
  5.143 on the
  eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-27/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-5/subpart-I/section-5.143).
  Here are the basics for bourbon:


  - The mash is at least 51% corn.

  - It is distilled to 160 proof (80% ABV) or less.

  - It enters new charred oak barrels at 125 proof or less.

  - No coloring, flavoring, or blending materials can be added.


  Rye follows the same distillation and barrel rules, but its mash must be at
  least 51% rye. Labels that say 'straight' on either style require at least two
  years in new charred oak.


  Those rules are the floor, not the whole story. They tell you what a bottle
  must be, not where it sits between the minimum and the maximum. That gap is
  where the glass changes.

  ## Why the Mash Bill Matters More Than the 51% Line


  A mash bill is the grain recipe that goes into the fermenter. Corn, rye,
  wheat, and malted barley are the usual players. Corn is the majority in any
  bourbon, and it is the main reason bourbon tastes sweet. The secondary grain
  fills the rest of the mash, and it shapes the spice or softness on top.


  Most bourbon sits well above the legal minimum. Bourbon guides put typical
  corn content somewhere between 60 and 75 percent, and [Bourbon
  Excursions](https://www.bourbonexcursions.com/bourbon-blog/what-makes-a-whiskey-a-bourbon)
  cites 65 to 75 percent for Kentucky distilleries. That leaves a lot of room
  for the secondary grain to matter.


  ### Bourbon's Secondary Grain: Rye or Wheat


  Bourbon with a meaningful rye share is often called high-rye. [Wine
  Enthusiast](https://www.wineenthusiast.com/basics/rye-vs-bourbon/) defines
  high-rye bourbon as one with more than 20% rye in the mash. These bottles
  usually taste sweet up front and spicier on the back end.


  Wheated bourbon swaps rye for wheat. Wheat tends to soften the profile, so
  wheated bottles usually read rounder and sweeter. Maker's Mark and Weller are
  two well-known examples.


  Bulleit Bourbon shows the high-rye approach clearly. Its mash bill is 68%
  corn, 28% rye, and 4% malted barley, according to [Wikipedia's entry on
  Bulleit Bourbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulleit_Bourbon). At 28% rye,
  it clears the high-rye threshold, which is why it tends to read spicier than a
  wheated bourbon.


  ### Rye's Range: From 51% to 95% and Beyond


  Rye doesn't come in one style. According to [Wikipedia's rye whiskey
  entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rye_whiskey), Pennsylvania-style rye has
  historically been upwards of 95% rye, Maryland-style used 65 to 70% rye, and
  Kentucky-style is normally 51 to 55% rye. Those are very different starting
  points.


  Bulleit Rye is 95% rye and 5% malted barley, which puts it at the high end of
  that range. Wine Enthusiast notes that mash bills at 95% rye or more produce
  noticeably spicier whiskey than a 51% rye with a lot of corn.


  The categories overlap more than the labels suggest. A high-rye bourbon can
  taste spicier than a rye that sits near the legal minimum, because the grain
  share is only one piece of the glass. Barrel char, proof, and age push the
  flavor around too.

  ## Named Bottles to Compare Side by Side


  Start with one brand so the distillery style stays close, then compare these
  bottles:


  - **Bulleit Bourbon:** 68% corn, 28% rye, and 4% malted barley. It's a good
  first step into high-rye bourbon, with enough sweetness to stay approachable.

  - **Maker's Mark:** a wheated bourbon. Pour it beside a high-rye bourbon and
  the difference in spice should be easy to spot.

  - **Weller:** another wheated bourbon, generally softer and sweeter. Use it as
  a reference point for how much of the spice comes from rye.

  - **Bulleit Rye:** 95% rye and 5% malted barley. This is the bottle to try if
  you want to know what a very rye-heavy mash tastes like.


  Not everyone wants the spice. Some drinkers find high-rye bourbon too sharp
  for sipping, while others find wheated bottles flat. Neither preference is
  wrong. Our opinion is that the best bottle is the one you'll reach for again.

  ## Is Bourbon Sweeter Than Rye?


  Usually, yes. Corn-heavy mash bills tend to read sweeter, and rye-heavy ones
  tend to read drier. Charred oak adds vanilla and caramel notes to both, so age
  can make a bottle feel sweeter than its grain alone would suggest. The main
  exception is high-rye bourbon, which can taste spicier than a rye near the
  legal minimum.


  ## Which Is Better for an Old Fashioned or a Manhattan?


  One cocktail guide from The Liquor Barn describes bourbon as making a rounder,
  sweeter Manhattan. Our take, which is opinion rather than rule:


  - **Old Fashioned:** Bourbon is the easier pick if you want a softer, sweeter
  drink. A high-rye bourbon adds more bite without losing body.

  - **Manhattan:** Rye is the classic choice if you want a drier, sharper drink
  with more backbone.


  You can swap them, but expect a different drink. Bourbon in a Manhattan tastes
  rounder, and rye in an Old Fashioned tastes drier and spicier.


  ## How to Run a Simple Bourbon vs Rye Tasting


  You don't need special equipment. Here's a quick method:


  1. Choose two bottles from one brand, such as Bulleit Bourbon and Bulleit Rye,
  plus one wheated bourbon as a third reference.

  2. Check the proof on each label. If the numbers differ a lot, note it, since
  the higher-proof bottle will taste hotter.

  3. Pour equal amounts, neat, into identical glasses at room temperature.

  4. Smell each glass first, then take a small sip and note whether sweetness or
  spice comes first.

  5. Rate the finish. Write one word for sweetness, one for spice, and one for
  how long the flavor lasts.

  6. Between pours, sip water and eat a plain cracker to reset your palate.


  Done in order, this gives you a clear sense of how the grain shifts the glass,
  without relying on anyone else's tasting notes.

  ## Frequently Asked Questions


  ### Is Rye Whiskey a Bourbon?


  No. Bourbon requires a mash of at least 51% corn, while rye requires at least
  51% rye. A single mash can't meet both requirements, so rye is a separate
  category, even though both follow similar distilling and barrel rules.


  ### Can Bourbon Contain Rye?


  Yes. Bourbon can use rye as its secondary grain, and high-rye bourbons do
  exactly that, as long as corn stays at 51% or more.


  ### Does Bourbon Have to Be Made in Kentucky?


  No. The federal definition doesn't require Kentucky, although much of the
  bourbon in the world is made there.


  ### Is Rye Harsher Than Bourbon?


  Rye usually tastes drier and spicier, which some people read as harsher.
  Strength depends on the proof printed on the label, not on the grain.


  ### Can You Substitute Bourbon for Rye in Cocktails?


  You can, but the drink changes. Bourbon brings more sweetness and body, while
  rye brings more spice and dryness.


  ## The Takeaway


  Bourbon is corn-forward and usually sweeter. Rye is grain-forward in a
  different direction and usually spicier. The real range sits inside each
  category, so check the mash bill when you can, and use a high-rye bourbon or a
  wheated bottle to fine-tune what you want in the glass.
