  Most best productivity tools for developers roundups hand you fifteen apps and
  leave you to sort them out. If you work on a Mac, a better approach is to
  start with the problem you need to solve. For most individual developers, the
  best productivity tools for developers on a Mac come down to four jobs: a
  launcher, a terminal, a notes app, and a task list that can handle side
  projects. Team tools only matter when other people depend on your work.


  Below, we group the options by problem and give a verdict for each developer
  profile, so you can jump straight to the section that matches your week.


  ## How we picked these tools


  Our criteria are simple. Each tool has to run on macOS, solve one clear
  problem, and make sense for a solo developer before it makes sense for a team.
  We also favor tools with a usable free tier or a plan whose price is easy to
  check.


  Prices change often, so confirm them on the vendor's site before you buy. On
  3minread, we would rather recommend a smaller stack you will actually keep
  than a long list you abandon after a week.


  ## Tasks: track what you owe


  Tasks answer one question: what do I need to do next? Keep this list separate
  from your notes, or it turns into a graveyard of half-finished ideas.


  ### Super Productivity


  Super Productivity is an open-source task manager that runs on macOS and is
  released under the MIT license, so the code is open and free to use. It is one
  of the few options that suits juggling a day job with side projects, which
  makes it useful if your evenings belong to a personal app. For a comparison
  with other open-source options, see the [Super Productivity open-source
  roundup](https://super-productivity.com/blog/open-source-productivity-apps-comparison/).


  **Verdict:** Best for developers with a day job and side projects who want
  tasks that stay close to the work.


  ### Taskwarrior


  Taskwarrior is a command-line task manager, also MIT-licensed, with a macOS
  command-line version. You add, filter, and complete tasks from the shell, so
  your to-do list lives where you already type.


  **Verdict:** A strong pick if you live in the terminal. Skip it if typing
  commands feels like friction.


  ### Linear and Jira


  Linear and Jira are issue trackers built for teams. They are powerful for
  shared sprints and tickets, and they are overkill for a solo developer.


  **Verdict:** Use them only if your team already runs its work there.
  Otherwise, keep your personal list in a lighter app.


  For a wider look at task apps beyond developer circles, our guide to the [best
  task managers for
  freelancers](https://www.3minread.com/task-manager-for-freelancers) compares
  five options on the same criteria.

  ## Notes: keep the reasoning, not just the to-do


  Notes hold what tasks cannot: why you chose a library, the command you will
  forget by Friday, or the meeting decision you need to revisit. Here is how
  three popular apps handle that job.


  ### Obsidian


  Obsidian stores notes as plain Markdown files in a folder on your Mac. That
  makes it easy to link ideas together and keep project notes next to your code.


  **Verdict:** Best for developers who write a lot of project documentation and
  want their notes to stay portable.


  ### Joplin


  Joplin has a terminal application that lets you search and edit notes without
  leaving a command-line workflow. If most of your day happens in the shell,
  that matters.


  **Verdict:** Pick Joplin when the terminal is where you spend most of your
  time.


  ### Notion


  Notion is strongest for shared docs, wikis, and team databases. For solo
  notes, it can feel heavier than you need.


  **Verdict:** A good choice for teams that want one shared workspace. A slower
  fit for solo note-taking.


  A simple rule helps: tasks answer what to do next, notes answer what you
  learned, and an issue tracker answers what the team agreed to ship. If a note
  starts looking like a task, move it.

  ## Launchers and window management


  A launcher saves you from hunting through folders and menus. Window tools stop
  you from dragging windows around by hand. Many developers get both in one app,
  so check what you already have before you install several.


  ### Raycast


  Raycast's free plan includes window management, snippets, and clipboard
  history. On the free plan, clipboard history reaches back three months. Pro is
  listed at $96 a year, or $10 a month, and adds Raycast AI with a monthly
  allowance, according to [Tools.us.org's Raycast
  page](https://tools.us.org/tools/raycast/). Plans change, so check the current
  price on Raycast's site before you buy.


  **Verdict:** The free plan covers most solo developers. Pay for Pro only if
  you use its AI features every day.


  ### Alfred


  Alfred is the other launcher that appears in most Mac comparisons. It handles
  app launching, file search, and custom workflows.


  **Verdict:** Stick with Alfred if you already rely on its workflows.
  Otherwise, pick one launcher and learn it well rather than running two.


  ### Rectangle and Magnet


  Both tools snap windows into halves, thirds, and corners using keyboard
  shortcuts.


  **Verdict:** If your launcher already manages windows, you may not need
  either. For switching between open apps, our guide to [Alt + Tab window
  switching on
  Mac](https://www.3minread.com/master-window-switching-on-mac-how-to-use-alt-tab-with-one-powerful-app)
  covers one approach.


  ### CopyClip


  CopyClip is a clipboard history app.


  **Verdict:** Skip it if Raycast's clipboard history covers your needs, which
  it does for recent items.


  ## Terminal: stay on Terminal.app or switch?


  You spend hours in your terminal, so small friction adds up. Start with what
  you already have, then switch only when you hit a clear limit.


  ### Terminal.app with Oh My Zsh


  Oh My Zsh is a framework that loads plugins, themes, and autocompletion on top
  of your current shell. You keep the app you already have and gain better tab
  completion and useful aliases.


  **Verdict:** The best first step for most Mac developers, with no new app to
  install.


  ### iTerm2


  iTerm2 is a long-established alternative to Terminal.app with deeper
  customization options.


  **Verdict:** Switch when you find yourself fighting Terminal.app's limits, not
  because a list told you to.


  ### Warp


  Warp is a modern terminal. The individual plan is free, while Teams is listed
  at $15 per user per month, according to [Lifestack's developer productivity
  guide](https://lifestack.ai/blog/developer-productivity-tools).


  **Verdict:** Try Warp if you want a more modern interface and the free
  individual plan covers your needs.


  **The short decision:** Stay on Terminal.app for a handful of commands a day,
  add Oh My Zsh for autocomplete, and move to iTerm2 or Warp only if a specific
  limit slows you down.

  ## AI coding assistants: pick one, not five


  AI assistants are the largest category in most roundups, and they are easy to
  over-buy. Cursor is built around the editor, so it suits developers who want
  AI inside the code window. Claude Code suits developers who work mainly in the
  terminal. GitHub Copilot is the familiar choice, with Copilot Pro listed at
  $10 a month and Business at $19 per seat, according to [Catio's developer
  tools guide](https://www.catio.tech/blog/developer-productivity-tools). The
  same guide lists Cursor's team plan at $40 per user.


  **Verdict:** Choose the tool that matches where you already work. Do not pay
  for two assistants that do the same job.

  ## Which stack fits your profile


  ### Solo developer with one main project


  Start with Raycast Free, Terminal.app with Oh My Zsh, and Obsidian for project
  notes. Skip the team tools. You will get most of the value without adding a
  subscription.


  ### Developer with side projects


  Add Super Productivity for tasks and keep one notes space for each side
  project. Separating day-job tasks from side-project tasks keeps evenings from
  blurring into work.


  ### Terminal-first developer


  Use iTerm2 or Warp, add Taskwarrior for tasks in the shell, and use Joplin if
  you want your notes in the terminal too.


  ### Team lead


  Use Linear or Jira for shared work, keep a personal notes app for your own
  planning, and decide as a group whether an AI assistant belongs in the shared
  budget.

  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### Is Raycast free?


  Yes. The free plan includes window management, snippets, and clipboard
  history, with clipboard history limited to three months. Pro adds AI features
  for a monthly or annual fee.


  ### Warp or iTerm2: which is better on a Mac?


  Neither wins for everyone. iTerm2 suits people who want deep customization in
  an established app. Warp suits people who want a modern interface and can live
  with its free individual plan. Start with Terminal.app and Oh My Zsh, then
  switch if you hit a real limit.


  ### Do solo developers need project management tools?


  Usually not a team tool. A task app and a notes app cover most solo work. Use
  Linear or Jira only when other people depend on your tickets.


  ### Are there free, open-source options that compete with paid tools?


  Yes. Super Productivity and Taskwarrior are both released under the MIT
  license, and both run on macOS. They will not replace a team tracker, but they
  handle personal and side-project work well.


  ### How long does it take to refocus after an interruption?


  A figure often cited from Gloria Mark's UC Irvine research is about 23
  minutes. Accounts of that number trace it to a 2006 Gallup Business Journal
  interview with Mark rather than a peer-reviewed paper, so treat it as a rough
  estimate, not a fixed rule. For a plain-language summary, see [this overview
  of refocus
  times](https://www.tryhugo.app/blog/how-long-to-refocus-after-interruption).


  ## The bottom line


  The best productivity tools for developers on a Mac are the ones that solve a
  specific problem you have this week. Start with a launcher, a terminal upgrade
  only if you need one, one notes app, and one task list that fits your
  workload. Add paid AI or team tools only when they replace work you currently
  do by hand every day.
