  To back up an iPhone, you have two main routes: iCloud Backup, which sends
  your data to Apple's servers over Wi-Fi, or a local backup to a Mac using
  Finder, which saves a copy to your computer. Both work. They differ on
  storage, speed, and privacy, and those three factors should decide which one
  you use. Below, we compare them and finish with a verdict for four common
  setups.


  ## How to Back Up an iPhone to iCloud


  iCloud Backup runs on its own once it is set up. [Apple
  says](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108366) it starts daily when your iPhone
  is connected to power, locked, and on Wi-Fi. You can also start one yourself:


  1. Open **Settings** and tap your name at the top.

  2. Tap **iCloud**, then **iCloud Backup**.

  3. Turn on **iCloud Backup**.

  4. Tap **Back Up Now**, and stay on Wi-Fi with the phone plugged in until it
  finishes.


  ## How to Back Up an iPhone to a Mac


  On a Mac, Finder handles iPhone backups. You need a reasonably recent version
  of macOS.


  1. Connect your iPhone with a cable and unlock it. If prompted, trust the
  computer.

  2. Open Finder and select your iPhone in the sidebar.

  3. In the General section, choose **Back up all of the data on your iPhone to
  this Mac**.

  4. Check **Encrypt local backup** and set a password you will remember. We
  explain why below.

  5. Click **Back Up Now**.

  ## Space: Run the Numbers Before You Decide


  iCloud Backup shows how much iCloud storage your backups use and how much is
  left. It also shows the size of the last backup, the next backup, and the apps
  included. Use that screen as your starting point. The number you need is the
  size of the next backup, not the total storage on your phone.


  The biggest variable is photos. When iCloud Photos is on, your photos and
  videos are not part of the iCloud Backup, because they already live in your
  iCloud library. That can make the backup much smaller than the storage your
  phone reports.


  Here is a rough example, not a measurement. Say your iPhone has 256 GB in use,
  and 180 GB of that is photos and videos. With iCloud Photos on, those 180 GB
  sit in your iCloud library, and the backup covers the rest of the phone, minus
  items that already sync to iCloud. The backup could be far smaller than 256
  GB, but your iCloud plan still has to hold the photo library. Add both numbers
  together before you pick a plan.


  A Mac backup has no iCloud limit. The trade-off is that it uses space on your
  computer's drive, so check free space before you start. It also skips some
  store content. Apple says purchases from the iTunes Store, App Store, and
  Apple Books are not included, so you will need to download them again from
  your account.

  ## Speed: What We Can Say (and What We Can't)


  Apple does not publish a speed benchmark for either method. An iCloud backup
  depends on your Wi-Fi upload speed. A Mac backup depends on your cable and
  drive. Most speed claims online are anecdotal.
  [Intego](https://www.intego.com/mac-security-blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-backups-and-storage-for-iphone-and-ipad/)
  says a full restore from a Mac can take half an hour or less, while an iCloud
  restore can take several hours or more. That is one vendor's claim, so treat
  it as something to test, not a rule.


  ### A Simple Test You Can Run


  - Back up your iPhone to iCloud over Wi-Fi and note the start time, end time,
  and backup size.

  - Connect the same iPhone to your Mac by cable and run a full Finder backup.
  Note the same details.

  - Restore a test device or spare device from each backup if you can, and time
  the process.


  Run the first full backup of each type to get a fair comparison, since later
  backups copy only what changed.


  ## Privacy: Three Levels, Not Two


  Most guides treat privacy as a yes-or-no question. In practice, there are
  three options, and they protect your data differently.


  ### Standard iCloud Backup


  Apple encrypts iCloud Backup, but under standard protection Apple holds the
  keys. That is a reasonable default for many people, but it is the least
  private of the three options.


  ### Advanced Data Protection


  Advanced Data Protection (ADP) turns on end-to-end encryption for iCloud
  Backup and nine other data categories, including Photos and Notes. [Apple's
  security
  overview](https://support.apple.com/guide/security/security-of-icloud-backup-sec2c21e7f49/web)
  explains that when ADP is on, iCloud Backup and everything inside it is
  end-to-end encrypted. Before you turn it on, Apple walks you through setting
  up a recovery contact or recovery key. Store that key somewhere safe. With
  end-to-end encryption, losing both your trusted devices and your recovery
  method can lock you out of your data.


  ### Encrypted Local Backup on a Mac


  A Mac backup is not encrypted by default. [Apple
  notes](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108353) that an encrypted local backup
  can include saved passwords, Wi-Fi settings, Health data, and call history,
  which is why it is worth turning on. Keep the password in a password manager.
  If you lose it, you cannot restore that backup.

  ## Sync Is Not a Backup, Especially on a Mac


  If you use a Mac, do not assume iCloud covers it. iCloud syncs files and
  photos across your devices, but it does not back up your Mac. [Backblaze makes
  the point](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/dont-get-trapped-icloud/) that
  deleting a photo on your Mac can remove it from your iPhone too. Syncing
  copies your mistakes as well as your files. A Mac user with iCloud still needs
  a separate backup of the computer itself, such as Time Machine to an external
  drive.


  ## Which Method Should You Choose? Verdicts by Profile


  ### Single iPhone, No Mac


  Use iCloud Backup. It is automatic, needs no computer, and protects you if
  your phone is lost, damaged, or replaced. If your photo library is small, the
  free tier may be enough. For most people, a paid plan is the real cost, so
  check the current price in Settings before you upgrade.


  ### Mac Owner With a Small iCloud Plan


  Make Finder your main backup. It costs nothing beyond the drive you already
  own, and you choose when it runs. Keep iCloud Backup on for the times you are
  away from your Mac. If space is tight, check whether iCloud Photos is taking
  most of your plan, because that is usually the cause. If you are still
  choosing a Mac for this setup, compare your options in [Mac mini vs MacBook
  Air: Which Is Better for
  Programming?](https://www.3minread.com/mac-mini-vs-macbook-air-programming).


  ### Privacy-Focused User


  Use an encrypted local backup on your Mac as your primary copy. If you also
  want a cloud copy, turn on Advanced Data Protection after you have stored your
  recovery key safely. In our view, standard iCloud Backup is the weakest choice
  of the three for privacy.


  ### Family With a Large Photo Library


  Plan your iCloud storage around the full photo library, not the backup alone.
  Photos are excluded from the iCloud Backup when iCloud Photos is on, but they
  still count against your plan. If the library is too large for a practical
  plan, a Mac backup can cover the rest of the phone's data while the library
  stays in iCloud for access across devices.

  ## Frequently Asked Questions


  ### Can I Back Up My iPhone Without Wi-Fi?


  Yes, with a Mac. A Finder backup runs over a cable and does not need Wi-Fi.
  iCloud Backup needs a Wi-Fi connection.


  ### Does iCloud Back Up My Photos?


  Not when iCloud Photos is on. Your photos and videos are already stored in
  your iCloud library, so they are excluded from the backup.


  ### Is the Free 5 GB Enough?


  For most iPhone users, probably not. The free 5 GB is shared across iCloud
  Photos, iCloud Backup, iCloud Drive, Mail, and other apps, so your backup
  competes with everything else.


  ### Is My iCloud Backup Encrypted?


  Yes. Apple encrypts iCloud Backup, and Advanced Data Protection adds
  end-to-end encryption on top of that.


  ### Should I Back Up to Both iCloud and a Mac?


  For most people, yes. iCloud gives you automatic protection, and a Mac backup
  gives you a full local copy you can restore without an internet connection.


  ## Takeaway


  Choose iCloud if you want automatic backups with no computer involved. Choose
  a Mac if you want control over timing, no storage fees, and a local copy you
