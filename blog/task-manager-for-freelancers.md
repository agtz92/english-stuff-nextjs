  The best task manager for freelancers is the one that keeps every client
  project visible and tells you where you left off after a break. For most solo
  workers, Todoist is the simplest starting point, Trello suits people who think
  in visual boards, and ClickUp, Asana, and Bonsai make sense when you need more
  structure, dependencies, or invoicing. Below, we compare five options on the
  three things that matter most: how they organize client work, what they cost,
  and how well they help you pick up work after a gap.


  ## Why resuming work matters as much as planning


  Missed deadlines rarely come from a lack of planning. They often come from
  losing the thread. Research on task interruption points the same way. In one
  study, the resumption lag, meaning the time it takes to restart a task, was
  about double the interval between uninterrupted actions: 3.8 seconds versus
  1.9 seconds. The same work found that cues available just before an
  interruption made it faster to get going again. See [Task Interruption:
  Resumption Lag and the Role of
  Cues](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/2909216_Task_Interruption_Resumption_Lag_and_the_Role_of_Cues).


  A [book chapter on how interruptions affect
  productivity](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4842-4221-6_9)
  treats a longer resumption lag as a sign of lower overall productivity.
  Individual differences matter too. A [PubMed study on working-memory capacity
  and task resumption](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26882286/) explores why
  some people pick interrupted work back up faster than others.


  For a freelancer, every gap between client sessions is an interruption. A tool
  that saves context, such as clear notes on the next step, is doing real work
  for you.

  ## How we judged the five


  These are feature-level criteria, applied to each tool's documented
  capabilities:


  - **Client project setup:** Can you run one project or board per client, with
  stages from brief to delivery?

  - **Pricing:** What the free plan includes, and what one person pays for the
  paid tier.

  - **Resuming after a gap:** Does the tool surface overdue or stalled work,
  store notes on where you stopped, and stay usable with patchy connectivity?

  - **Freelancer workflows:** Can you handle approvals, subcontractors, and file
  versions without adding a second app?


  ## 1. Todoist: best for solo freelancers with a few clients


  Todoist is a light, fast task app. Its free plan includes five personal
  projects, Smart Quick Add for typing tasks in plain language, and list or
  board layouts. According to Todoist's [official pricing and billing
  FAQ](https://www.todoist.com/help/account-and-billing/plans/todoist-plans-pricing-and-billing-faq-Vq2z0HWL6),
  Pro costs $7 per month or $60 per year, and Business costs $10 per user per
  month or $8 per user per month billed yearly.


  For client work, create one project per client and use sections for stages
  such as Brief, Drafting, Review, and Delivered. Filters help you spot overdue
  tasks, which is the core of getting back on track after a break.


  The catch is that the five-project cap on the free plan fills quickly if you
  juggle many clients. There is also no dedicated space for client files or
  sign-offs, so you will rely on task notes and links.


  **Our verdict:** the strongest pick for a solo freelancer with a handful of
  active clients who wants to spend more time on the work than on the tool.


  ## 2. Trello: best for visual per-client boards


  Trello organizes work as cards on boards, which suits freelancers who like to
  see everything at a glance. Make one board per client and use the columns as
  stages: Brief, Drafting, Review, Approved, Delivered. Move cards across as the
  project moves forward.


  Agiled's [roundup of project management tools for
  freelancers](https://agiled.app/blog/best-project-management-tools-for-freelancers)
  counts Trello's free plan among the most capable options for pure task
  management.


  For resuming work, the board does the heavy lifting. A card that has sat in
  Review for a week is visible without opening anything. The limit is that time
  tracking, invoicing, and advanced reporting usually come through add-ons or
  paid tiers, so check what you need before you commit.


  **Our verdict:** a good choice if you think visually and manage a few clients
  at once.

  ## 3. ClickUp: best for structured workflows


  ClickUp is the most configurable tool on this list. You can organize work into
  spaces for each client, then add lists, docs, and timeline or Gantt views.
  Agiled's FAQ names ClickUp's Free Forever plan as one of the most capable free
  options for pure task management.


  The strength is also the trap. Setup takes time, and a freelancer with two
  clients can end up maintaining the system instead of doing the work. For
  resumption, dashboards and saved views can surface overdue and blocked items,
  but only after you build them.


  **Our verdict:** a strong fit if you want tasks, docs, and timelines in one
  workspace and don't mind spending an afternoon on setup.


  ## 4. Asana: best for task dependencies


  Asana's standout feature is dependencies. If a client must approve a draft
  before you start the final design, you can link those tasks so the next one
  unlocks when the first is done. That makes it easy to see what is blocked
  after a break.


  Asana offers a free Personal plan for individuals. Before you commit, test how
  it behaves offline. Working from cafés, trains, or planes exposes a gap that
  many listicles skip, and a tool that stalls without a connection is a poor fit
  for picking work back up in those places.


  **Our verdict:** best if your client projects have many dependent steps and
  you usually work on a stable connection.


  ## 5. Bonsai: best for running client work end to end


  Bonsai is built for freelancers rather than adapted from team software. It
  pairs project tracking with client-facing tools and invoicing, so you can move
  from proposal to delivery to payment without switching apps. Pricing is
  plan-based, so check the current page before you buy.


  The trade-off is cost in attention and money. If you only want a list of
  tasks, you will pay for features you don't use, and a stand-alone task app
  will be cheaper and simpler.


  **Our verdict:** the right pick if you invoice clients and want their
  information, projects, and payments in one place.

  ## Does per-seat pricing matter when you work alone?


  For a solo freelancer, per-seat pricing is wasted money, because collaboration
  features go unused. The math changes once you bring in a subcontractor or give
  a client access. Calculate the cost for one user first, then for the number of
  people you expect to add within a year.


  ## How do you handle subcontractors, approvals, and file versions?


  Few task managers handle these well. Many freelancers keep files in a cloud
  drive and use the task manager only for status. That split works if every task
  links to the current file version, so the latest draft is one click away. If
  you hire help, check whether guest access is included and whether your task
  manager can keep feedback attached to the right card.


  The main risk on both sides is complexity. A team-grade tool can make a solo
  workflow heavier than it needs to be, while a solo-grade app may struggle the
  moment a second person joins.

  ## How to test resumption yourself


  Before you pay for anything, run a simple check:


  1. Pick one real client project with at least eight tasks, two deadlines, and
  one stalled item.

  2. Set it up in the tool you're considering, including a note on the next
  step.

  3. Step away from it for a full week.

  4. Come back and time how long it takes you to make your first concrete move.

  5. Note whether the tool told you what changed, what is overdue, and what
  comes next.


  Repeat the check with no more than two tools. If you prefer to build your own
  system, our guide to [turning Notion into a to-do list
  manager](https://www.3minread.com/how-to-turn-notion-into-the-ultimate-to-do-list-manager-template-included)
  shows one template-based approach.


  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### Is there a free task manager for freelancers?


  Yes. Todoist's free plan includes five personal projects, and Trello and
  ClickUp both offer free plans that cover basic task management. If you would
  rather build your own setup, see our guide to [turning Notion into a to-do
  list
  manager](https://www.3minread.com/how-to-turn-notion-into-the-ultimate-to-do-list-manager-template-included).
  Free tiers are a good way to test your workflow before paying.


  ### Do freelancers need team project management software?


  Usually not. Team suites add collaboration features that solo workers rarely
  use. They become worth the cost when clients, approvers, or subcontractors
  need regular access.


  ### What should a freelance project management app include?


  At minimum, tasks, deadlines, a way to organize work by client, and a record
  of where each project stands. Time tracking, file handling, and invoicing are
  useful extras if they match how you work.


  ### How many projects does Todoist's free plan allow?


  The free plan is capped at five personal projects. If you manage more than
  five active clients, you will need to archive finished work or move to a paid
  plan.


  ## Our verdict


  For most solo freelancers, we'd start with Todoist's free plan, move to Trello
  if you need to see client work visually, and consider ClickUp, Asana, or
  Bonsai only when your workflow genuinely needs their structure. Match the tool
  to your client count and working style, not to the longest feature list.


  Set up one real client project, step away for a week, and see how quickly you
  can get back into it. That single test will tell you more than any comparison
  table.
