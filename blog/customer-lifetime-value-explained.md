  Customer lifetime value explained simply: it is the total profit you expect a
  customer to generate over their entire relationship with your business. You
  can estimate it with four numbers most small businesses already track, one
  formula, and a few clear assumptions. Here is how it works, with a worked
  example you can copy.


  Customer lifetime value, usually shortened to CLV, answers a practical
  question: how much is a typical customer really worth? That answer shows how
  much you can afford to spend to win a new customer and where retention work
  pays off.


  ## What customer lifetime value means in plain English


  CLV is a prediction. It estimates what a customer will be worth to you across
  the whole relationship, from the first purchase to the last.
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_lifetime_value) describes
  it as an estimate and prediction of net profit, and treats it as an upper
  limit on what you should spend to acquire a new customer. The idea is older
  than most software: one of the first published accounts appears in the 1988
  book *Database Marketing*.


  Some sources quote CLV as revenue, meaning what customers spend. That figure
  is bigger and easier to calculate, but it overstates what you keep. For
  decisions about ad budgets or discounts, profit is the more useful version, so
  check which one any number uses.

  ## The simple CLV formula


  The most common version for a small business looks like this:


  **CLV = average order value × purchases per year × customer lifespan in years
  × gross margin**


  - **Average order value:** what a typical customer spends per purchase.

  - **Purchases per year:** how often that customer buys in a 12-month period.

  - **Customer lifespan:** how many years the relationship lasts on average.

  - **Gross margin:** the share of each sale left after the direct cost of the
  product, written as a decimal. A 60% margin is 0.60.


  Leave out the margin and you have the revenue version. [Twilio's customer
  lifetime value
  guide](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/insights/customer-lifetime-value-clv)
  shows the gap: a $75 average purchase, bought three times a year for four
  years, equals $900 in revenue. At a 25% margin, the same customer is worth
  $225 in profit.


  Subscription and membership businesses often swap lifespan for churn.
  [CleverTap's CLV guide](https://clevertap.com/blog/customer-lifetime-value/)
  explains that a 25% annual churn rate implies an average lifespan of four
  years (1 ÷ 0.25 = 4). Use whichever input you can measure more reliably.


  ## A worked example with stated assumptions


  Imagine a neighborhood coffee roaster that sells bags of beans online. This
  example is hypothetical, built to show the math. Here are the inputs:


  <table>

  <thead><tr><th>Input</th><th>Assumption</th><th>Basis</th></tr></thead>

  <tbody><tr><td>Average order value</td><td>$14</td><td>Typical single order,
  excluding shipping</td></tr><tr><td>Purchases per
  year</td><td>20</td><td>About one order every two to three
  weeks</td></tr><tr><td>Customer lifespan</td><td>3 years</td><td>Estimate from
  18 months of order history</td></tr><tr><td>Gross
  margin</td><td>60%</td><td>Sales minus the cost of beans, bags, and
  packing</td></tr></tbody>

  </table>


  Revenue CLV is $14 × 20 × 3 = $840. Profit CLV is $840 × 0.60 = $504. Each new
  customer is worth about $504 in gross profit over three years, which is the
  ceiling for what the roaster could spend to acquire one.


  Changing one input at a time shows what moves the answer:


  <table>

  <thead><tr><th>Scenario (one change from base)</th><th>Revenue
  CLV</th><th>Profit CLV</th></tr></thead>

  <tbody><tr><td>Base case</td><td>$840</td><td>$504</td></tr><tr><td>Fewer
  orders: 12 per year</td><td>$504</td><td>$302</td></tr><tr><td>Shorter
  relationship: 2 years</td><td>$560</td><td>$336</td></tr><tr><td>Lower margin:
  50%</td><td>$840</td><td>$420</td></tr><tr><td>Larger average order:
  $18</td><td>$1,080</td><td>$648</td></tr></tbody>

  </table>


  The table makes one point clear. A drop in order frequency hurts more than a
  small margin change, which is why retention work often pays off first.

  ## What happens when one big order skews the numbers


  Averages are fragile when your customer base is small. Suppose 39 customers
  average $14 per order, and one customer places a single $300 catering order.
  The overall average jumps to about $21 (39 × $14 + $300 = $846, divided by 40
  orders gives $21.15). Plugging $21 into the formula lifts profit CLV from $504
  to roughly $756, all because of one outlier.


  Two habits protect you. First, use the median order value, or remove one-off
  bulk orders from the average. Second, report a range instead of a single
  number, such as the base case and a conservative case. A range keeps decisions
  grounded when your sample is small.

  ## Is your CLV good? Compare it with acquisition cost


  CLV on its own tells you little. It becomes useful when you compare it with
  customer acquisition cost (CAC), the average amount you spend on ads,
  promotions, and sales time to win one new customer. [Adwave's small business
  guide](https://adwave.com/resources/customer-lifetime-value-small-business)
  gives a rough reading: a CLV-to-CAC ratio below 1:1 means you lose money on
  each customer you acquire, while a ratio above 5:1 can signal that you are
  underinvesting in growth.


  Suppose the roaster spends $50 to win each new customer. Against profit CLV of
  $504, the ratio is about 10:1, which suggests room to spend more on growth.
  Against revenue CLV, the ratio looks even better, and that is exactly why
  profit is the safer basis.


  You will also see a 3:1 rule of thumb in many guides. Treat it as a starting
  point rather than a law. Margins, purchase cycles, and cost structures differ
  across industries, so a healthy ratio for a software company may look nothing
  like one for a local shop.


  ## Why retention is the lever to watch


  CLV rises when customers stay longer, which makes retention the place where
  value compounds. A 2014 [Harvard Business Review
  article](https://hbr.org/2014/10/the-value-of-keeping-the-right-customers)
  cites Bain research finding that increasing customer retention rates by 5% can
  increase profits by 25% to 95%. The same article notes that acquiring a new
  customer can cost five to 25 times more than keeping an existing one.


  The range is wide because results depend on the business. Use the figure to
  frame a useful question, such as what one extra year of repeat orders would be
  worth, rather than as a forecast for your own store.

  ## The limits of CLV for small businesses


  CLV is a useful estimate, but it has real limits when you run a small team:


  - **Lifespan is a guess when data is thin.** A shop with six months of history
  cannot know its true customer lifespan. Start with a conservative estimate and
  recalculate as data builds up.

  - **Revenue-based numbers can overstate value.** If customers mostly buy
  discounted items or send orders back, a revenue-only CLV may be too high.

  - **Service businesses carry hidden costs.** If you are a consultant, cleaner,
  or tutor, your own time and your staff's time are part of the cost of serving
  each customer. Subtract those labor costs, or your margin will look better
  than it really is.

  - **Industries differ.** A software subscription, a furniture store, and a
  dental practice follow very different purchase patterns, so benchmarks do not
  transfer neatly.

  - **CLV is a forecast.** Present it as an estimate, and revisit it on a
  schedule.


  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### Is CLV the same as LTV?


  Most marketing writing uses CLV and LTV (lifetime value) to mean the same
  thing. The bigger issue is whether the figure is based on revenue or profit,
  so check the definition behind any number you compare.


  ### Should CLV be based on revenue or profit?


  Profit is the safer choice. Revenue shows money coming in, while profit shows
  what is left to cover acquisition, staff, and growth. If you use revenue,
  label it clearly.


  ### How often should you recalculate CLV?


  Adwave recommends at least once a year, and quarterly if your business is
  growing quickly. Quarterly updates also help you catch a drop in order
  frequency before it becomes a trend.


  ### Is CLV useful for a small business?


  Yes, as a planning tool rather than a precise score. It helps you decide how
  much to spend on acquisition, which customers to prioritize, and where
  retention efforts will pay off most.


  ## Conclusion


  Customer lifetime value explained in one line: it is the profit a customer
  generates over time. Calculate it with a clear formula, state every
  assumption, compare it with acquisition cost, and be honest about the limits.
  Start with the numbers you already have and refine them as your data grows.
