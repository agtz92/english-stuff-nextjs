  Lunar New Year traditions follow a simple logic: start the year with a clean
  home, eat foods whose names sound lucky, and pass out money in red envelopes.
  The holiday falls on Saturday, February 6, 2027, and it opens the Year of the
  Goat for families across China, Korea, Vietnam, Mongolia, and Indonesia. The
  details change from place to place, but the message is consistent: leave the
  old year behind and invite good fortune in.


  This guide covers the customs you will see most often, which are house
  cleaning, food, and red envelopes. For each one, we explain what it means and
  where it comes from. Japan is left out on purpose because it has its own

  ## Why people clean the house before Lunar New Year


  Sweeping is the most visible custom.
  [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chinese-New-Year) explains that
  families clean thoroughly to sweep out the old year's bad luck, traditionally
  around the 24th day of the last lunar month. The idea is simple: misfortune
  leaves through the door before the new year arrives.


  The wordplay makes it memorable. In Chinese, the word for dust sounds like the
  word for old, so clearing dust means clearing away the past year, according to
  China Highlights.


  ### Why you should wait to sweep


  Sweeping on New Year's Day is traditionally avoided, because you risk sweeping
  away the good luck you are hoping to welcome. Britannica advises against
  cleaning on New Year's Day for this reason.


  ### What families usually do before the holiday


  - Deep-clean the home in the days before the holiday, so the new year starts
  fresh.

  - Settle debts and finish unfinished business, a custom also noted in Taiwan.

  - Buy new clothes for the holiday, as many people do during imlek in
  Indonesia.

  - Avoid haircuts and breaking dishes in the first days of the year, since both
  can be read as bad omens.

  ## Food with a lucky twist


  Lunar New Year menus are built around food names that sound like good wishes.
  The best-known example is fish. According to Britannica, the Chinese word for
  fish sounds like the word for surplus, which signals abundance in the year
  ahead. Fish usually appears as a centerpiece of the holiday table.


  ### Korea: rice cake soup


  In Korea, the holiday is called Seollal and typically lasts two to three days.
  The signature dish is tteokguk, a soup made with sliced rice cakes.
  [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/chinese-new-year) says the soup
  symbolizes starting the year with a clean mind and body, which is why it is
  served at the start of the celebration.


  ### Vietnam: banh chung


  In Vietnam, Tết brings banh chung, a square cake of glutinous rice wrapped in
  banana leaves and filled with mung beans and pork. Families prepare it in the
  weeks before the holiday, and its square shape is said to represent the earth.


  ### China: dumplings


  In much of northern China, dumplings are the centerpiece of the holiday meal.
  Families often make them together, turning food prep into a shared task that
  starts the year as a team.


  ## Red envelopes: money with a message


  Red envelopes are the most recognizable custom. [National Geographic
  Education](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/lunar-new-year/)
  notes that they are traditionally given to children and unmarried adults. Red
  is linked with luck and joy in Chinese culture, so the envelope doubles as a
  good-luck gift.

  ## Mongolia and Southeast Asia


  Mongolia celebrates Tsagaan Sar, a name that translates to White Moon.
  According to [Nopality
  Magazine](https://www.nopalitymag.com/arts-culture/how-lunar-new-year-is-celebrated-across-the-world),
  the white theme stands for purity as the new year begins.


  In Indonesia, imlek celebrations include visiting temples and washing the
  statues of deities, alongside buying new clothes. Nopality also covers these
  practices, which show how the holiday blends with local religious life.


  Taiwan adds a practical twist. Folk custom holds that debts should be settled
  before the new year begins, so the year starts with a clean financial slate.

  ## A naming debate you will hear about


  Many people still ask why it is called Lunar New Year and not Chinese New
  Year. The name is contested. Some prefer Lunar New Year because the holiday is
  celebrated across many cultures. Others keep Chinese New Year because the
  calendar and many customs trace back to China. Commentators are still debating
  the term, so both names appear in news coverage.


  Official recognition is also growing. In December 2024, UNESCO added China's
  Spring Festival to its Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage,
  according to [Chinese state
  media](https://english.www.gov.cn/news/202412/05/content_WS6750dd47c6d0868f4e8edab6.html).


  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### When is Lunar New Year 2027?


  Lunar New Year 2027 falls on Saturday, February 6, 2027. It marks the start of
  the Year of the Goat.


  ### Why do people clean before Lunar New Year?


  Cleaning sweeps out the bad luck of the old year. The dust-and-old wordplay in
  Chinese reinforces the idea of leaving the past behind.


  ### Is it bad luck to clean on New Year's Day?


  Many families avoid it. Britannica advises against cleaning on New Year's Day,
  since you could sweep away the good luck you hope to bring in.


  ### Do all countries use red envelopes?


  No. Red envelopes are common in China, Hong Kong, and Vietnam, but Korea uses
  white and patterned envelopes for New Year's money given by elders.


  ## Takeaway


  Lunar New Year traditions are about renewal. Clean the house, share a meal
  with lucky foods, and pass on a red envelope if you are giving. Pick one
  custom to try this year and learn its story. It is the quickest way to feel
  what the holiday means.
