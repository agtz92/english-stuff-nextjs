  ## Why most side project ideas stall, and how to pick one that ends


  Most side project ideas die for the same reason: they have no end. A project
  starts with energy, then drifts into a folder nobody opens. The fix is not a
  better idea. It is a smaller one with a finish line. Below are 10 side project
  ideas that take a weekend to about a month. Each one has a defined scope, a
  time estimate, and a stop point, the moment you ship and stop adding features.


  We wrote this list at 3minread for readers who have more half-finished
  projects than shipped ones. Most ideas use tools you probably already own:
  Google Sheets, Google Docs, a Mac, a mechanical keyboard, and a bottle or two
  of whisky for the tasting projects.


  ## Three rules to set before you start


  - **Define done in one sentence.** 'A one-page guide, published' is done. 'A
  better guide someday' is not.

  - **Cap the time, not just the scope.** Give each build a fixed number of
  evenings.

  - **Keep one active build at a time.** Several open projects make each one
  harder to finish.


  ## Does a plan actually help you finish?


  There is evidence behind the if-then habit. In a 2006 meta-analysis of 94
  independent tests, Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran found that
  implementation intentions, which are if-then plans that name the next action
  in advance, had a medium-to-large positive effect on goal attainment (d =
  .65). In practice, 'When I finish dinner, I log one bottle in the sheet' works
  better than 'I'll work on my tasting log soon.' The [Gollwitzer and Sheeran
  meta-analysis](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)
  is the study behind that idea.


  The pull to return to unfinished work is real, but the popular story is
  overstated. A 2025 [meta-analysis in Humanities and Social Sciences
  Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w) found no
  memory advantage for unfinished tasks. It did find a general tendency to
  resume interrupted work. That is the useful part. A half-built project keeps
  asking for attention, so a clear stop point lets you answer that pull on
  purpose instead of with guilt.

  ## 10 side project ideas you can finish this month


  Each entry lists the scope, the time, and the stop point. The stop point
  matters most, because it tells you when to stop.


  ### 1. A spirits tasting log in Google Sheets


  - **Scope:** One sheet with columns for distillery, style, age statement,
  price paid, nose, palate, finish, a 'would buy again' yes or no, and a score
  from 1 to 10.

  - **Time:** One or two evenings.

  - **Stop when:** You have logged 15 bottles and the columns stop changing.
  Skip charts until you have used the log for a month.


  Define what the scores mean before you start. A 7 might mean you would pour it
  again for a friend. Without that rule, your numbers will drift and stop being
  comparable.


  ### 2. An Islay versus Speyside guide in Google Docs


  - **Scope:** A single document comparing the two Scotch whisky regions, using
  only bottles you have actually tasted. Use a five-row table: peat, sweetness,
  body, finish, and best occasion.

  - **Time:** One afternoon.

  - **Stop when:** The document fits on one page. Share it as a link with one
  friend and call it finished.


  Islay is widely known for smoky, peated whiskies, while Speyside is often
  described as lighter and fruitier. Treat those descriptions as starting
  points, and let your own tasting notes do the rest. A guide built on your
  notes is more useful than a generic one, and it is short enough to finish.


  ### 3. A bourbon or rye pick guide


  - **Scope:** A decision sheet with three questions (sweeter or spicier, mixing
  or sipping, and budget) that point you toward a bourbon or a rye.

  - **Time:** Two evenings.

  - **Stop when:** The guide gives a clear answer for five common situations,
  such as a Manhattan, a neat pour, or a gift.


  The difference is worth stating in your guide. By US rules, bourbon must be at
  least 51% corn, and rye whiskey must be at least 51% rye. Rye tends to read
  drier and spicier, while bourbon often reads sweeter. Keep the guide focused
  on those criteria instead of brand rankings, and it will stay useful longer.


  ### 4. A keyboard parts budget in Google Sheets


  - **Scope:** One sheet listing every part for a single mechanical keyboard:
  case, PCB, plate, switches, keycaps, stabilizers, and cable. Add columns for
  price, status (to buy, ordered, arrived), and a running total.

  - **Time:** One evening to set up, then a few minutes each time a part
  arrives.

  - **Stop when:** The keyboard is assembled and the total is recorded. Do not
  start a second board until the first one types the way you want.


  The status column is the part that pays off. It stops the common problem of
  buying the same part twice because you forgot what was already on the way.


  ### 5. A keyboard maintenance log in Google Docs


  - **Scope:** A checklist for cleaning, lubing switches, and checking
  stabilizers, plus a dated entry for each service.

  - **Time:** About two hours.

  - **Stop when:** You have used the checklist once, from start to finish.
  Resist the urge to write a complete guide to every switch type.


  ### 6. A dotfiles repo for your Mac terminal


  - **Scope:** A Git repository with one shell configuration file, five aliases
  you use every day, and a short README.

  - **Time:** A weekend.

  - **Stop when:** You can clone the repo onto a second Mac and get your setup
  back in about 15 minutes. After that, stop tuning prompt colors.


  This is a good fit for coding on a Mac because the payoff is immediate. Each
  alias you actually use saves a few keystrokes every day, and the project has a
  clear, testable finish.


  ### 7. A screenshot sorter script for macOS


  - **Scope:** A short Python or shell script of about 30 to 50 lines that moves
  screenshots from your desktop into dated folders.

  - **Time:** One evening.

  - **Stop when:** It has run on your real desktop for a week without losing a
  file. Test on a copy of your folder first, and write any bugs in a notes file
  for later.


  ### 8. A weekly open-projects email with Google Apps Script


  - **Scope:** A Google Sheet listing your projects with a status column, plus a
  short Apps Script that emails you the Active ones every Monday using a
  time-based trigger.

  - **Time:** One evening.

  - **Stop when:** The email has arrived for two Mondays in a row. No dashboards
  and no login system.


  This one is a project about finishing projects, which makes it a useful first
  build if you keep starting and stopping.


  ### 9. A static portfolio page built on a Mac


  - **Scope:** One HTML file with a title, three project cards, and a contact
  link, hosted on a free static host such as GitHub Pages.

  - **Time:** A weekend.

  - **Stop when:** The page is live and linked from your profile. Do not add a
  framework or a blog until you have used the page for a month.


  ### 10. A typing test page in plain JavaScript


  - **Scope:** One HTML file that runs a 60-second typing test, counts words per
  minute, and shows the result. No accounts and no leaderboard.

  - **Time:** One or two evenings.

  - **Stop when:** Three test runs produce correct scores. It is tempting to add
  themes and high scores, so put those ideas in a second project instead.


  This build pairs well with the keyboard projects above, since it gives you a
  quick way to compare how a new board feels.

  ## How do you pick the right one?


  1. Choose the idea whose stop point you can write in one sentence.

  2. Write the scope in three bullets. Cut anything that does not serve the stop
  point.

  3. Block a calendar slot for each evening you will work on it.

  4. Copy the stop point to the top of your notes so you see it every session.

  5. When you reach it, share or publish the result, then close the project.

  ## Where do you track each idea?


  A tracker can help, but it carries a risk. It can become one more place to
  avoid finishing. Keep it to a single list and update it once a week.


  If you want one place for this, try [continuu.it](https://continuu.it), a
  project tool built for people who start more than they finish. Every project
  has a state: Active, Paused, Killed, or Completed, so unfinished work does not
  sit in limbo pretending it is still alive. Tasks live inside each project, so
  when you come back after three weeks you can see where you stopped. Notes keep
  the context you would otherwise have to rebuild, and ideas can be parked
  without becoming projects yet. Analytics show how much you start compared with
  how much you finish, and killed projects go to a Graveyard, because stopping a
  project on purpose is also a form of finishing. It is not a replacement for
  Notion as a docs or wiki tool, but it fits the job of tracking side projects.
  A free plan is available.


  ## Takeaway


  Pick one build from the list, write its stop point in a single sentence, and
  block two evenings this week. Ship it, share it, and then close it. A small
  finished project teaches you more than a large unfinished one, and it makes
  the next idea easier to start.


  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### How long should a side project take?


  For a small build, a weekend to one month is a sensible target. If a project
  runs past that, our rule of thumb is to cut scope rather than extend the
  deadline.


  ### Should I work on several side projects at once?


  Usually not. Several open projects split your attention, so keep one active
  build and mark the others as Paused with a short note about where you stopped.


  ### How do I know when a side project is done?


  It is done when it meets the stop point you wrote before you started. Any
  extra feature after that is a new project with its own scope.


  ### Are side projects worth it for getting hired?


  In our view, a small finished project with a live link or a clear result says
  more than an ambitious project that is still half-built. Choose the build that
  shows a finished result you can point to.


  ### Do I really need a tracker?


  Not always. A single sheet or document works for one or two projects. Once you
  have more than that, a tracker with clear states helps you see which projects
  are active and which are waiting.
