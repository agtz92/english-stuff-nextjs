  - **Free-plan limits:** Note counts, storage, devices, and file sizes.

  - **Search:** Can you find one note quickly in a large library?

  - **Export:** Can you leave with your notes in a format you can open somewhere
  else?

  ## Obsidian: one of the best free note-taking apps if you want files


  Obsidian saves your notes as plain Markdown files on your own device. The app
  is free for personal and commercial use, and you pay only for add-on services.
  Its official Sync service starts at $4 per user per month when billed
  annually, with 1 synced vault, 1 GB of total storage, a 5 MB maximum file
  size, and one month of version history, according to [Obsidian's Sync
  page](https://obsidian.md/sync). The [Standard plan
  announcement](https://obsidian.md/blog/standard-plan/) explains how the tiers
  fit together. The Plus tier raises the limits to 10 vaults and 10 GB.


  ### Free sync without paying


  You don't need Obsidian Sync to keep notes on two devices. Logseq can also
  sync for free this way. Here's a setup that works:


  1. Create your vault in a folder your cloud service already syncs, such as
  iCloud Drive on Apple devices or Google Drive on Windows and Android.

  2. On your second device, open the same synced folder as a vault in Obsidian.

  3. Run one sync method per vault. Using Obsidian Sync and a folder service
  together can create duplicate files.

  4. Let syncing finish before you switch devices, and avoid editing the same
  note on two devices while one is offline. Cloud services may save conflicting
  copies.

  5. If you're comfortable with Git, keep the vault in a private GitHub
  repository and pull changes on each device before you start writing.


  This setup is more hands-on than Obsidian Sync, but it costs nothing and keeps
  your files in a folder you control.


  ## Evernote: free, but the 50-note cap is tight


  Evernote is well known, but its free plan is the most restrictive of the best
  free note-taking apps. It allows up to 50 notes and 1 notebook, according to
  [Evernote's help
  center](https://help.evernote.com/hc/en-us/articles/23565231682323-Understanding-Evernote-Free-and-Starter-plans-limits-note-and-notebook-limits-explained).
  The paid Starter plan raises that to 1,000 notes and 20 notebooks. The free
  plan is enough to try the app, but not enough for a daily notebook. Device
  limits also apply, so check the current number on Evernote's pricing page
  before you rely on it across several devices.

  ## Notion: strong for solo users, tight for teams


  For one person, Notion's free plan offers unlimited blocks, which makes it a
  flexible personal wiki. Add a second workspace member and the free plan stops
  at 1,000 blocks. Deleting old content doesn't reset that counter. Uploads are
  capped at 5 MB, and page history covers about 7 days, according to
  [MyFlexNote's guide to free note
  apps](https://myflexnote.com/blog/best-free-note-taking-apps).


  If you're organizing projects and reading lists alone, the free plan is
  generous. If you need a shared space for a small team, plan for a paid tier
  early.


  ## OneNote: a student favorite among the best free note-taking apps


  OneNote's only hard limit on the free plan is 5 GB of OneDrive storage, shared
  across all Microsoft services, according to [Productivity Brief's student
  guide](https://productivitybrief.com/comparisons/free-note-taking-apps-students-2026-usable-plans).
  Notebooks, sections, and pages suit lecture notes well. OneNote can also
  search handwritten text through OCR, which helps if you take notes by hand on
  a tablet.


  The trade-off is storage. Five gigabytes disappears quickly if you store
  photos and recordings alongside your notes.


  ## Apple Notes and Google Keep: best inside one ecosystem


  Apple Notes is free and comes with 5 GB of iCloud storage. It's a strong
  choice if every device you use is from Apple. Its gaps are cross-platform
  support for Android and Windows, along with advanced audio and math tools.


  Google Keep shares 15 GB of storage with other Google apps. It's a simple pick
  for short notes and checklists, especially if you already use Gmail and Google
  Drive. Keep is less suited to long documents.


  ## Simplenote, Joplin, Notesnook, and Zoho Notebook


  These four are also worth knowing about among the best free note-taking apps:


  - **Simplenote:** Free, with no paid plan. Good for plain text that just needs
  to sync, with few extras.

  - **Joplin:** Open source. Sync requires setup unless you pay for Joplin
  Cloud, so it suits people who are comfortable configuring their tools.

  - **Notesnook:** The free tier offers unlimited notes and devices with basic
  sync, which makes it one of the most generous free plans here.

  - **Zoho Notebook:** The Essential free plan includes 2 GB of cloud storage
  per user, 100 MB file uploads, unlimited notes and notebooks, and sync across
  devices.


  Bear is a useful warning. You can write in it for free, but sync costs money,
  so it doesn't meet the sync criterion without a subscription.

  Most note apps are built around notebooks. If your real problem is starting
  many projects and losing the thread on each one, a project tool may fit
  better. [continuu.it](https://continuu.it) is a project tool for people who

  what it does:


  - Each project has a state: Active, Paused, Killed, or Completed, so nothing

  - Tasks live inside projects, so when you return after three weeks, you see
  where you stopped.

  - Notes stay attached to the project and keep the context you would otherwise
  have to rebuild.

  - Ideas can be parked without becoming a project yet.

  - Analytics compare how much you start against how much you finish.

  - Killed projects go to a Graveyard, because ending a project is also a way of
  finishing it.

  - Routines handle recurring work, and a calendar tracks real dates.


  [continuu.it](https://continuu.it) has a free plan and is built for
  freelancers, students with long projects, and side-project builders. It is not
  a replacement for Notion as a docs or wiki tool. If you need a shared
  knowledge base, keep Notion or Obsidian for that, and use
  [continuu.it](https://continuu.it) to track what you're actually working on.
  Check the site for current plan details, including sync and export, before you
  move your notes.


  ## Search and export: what to check before you commit


  Search and export are where the best free note-taking apps differ most, and
  they're easy to skip. Run these three checks in your first week, not after you
  have 500 notes:


  1. **Search:** Load at least 200 notes and search for a phrase from the middle
  of an old note. Time how long it takes to find it.

  2. **Export:** Export a set of notes, including attachments, to Markdown or
  PDF. Open the files on a different device.

  3. **Offline:** Turn off Wi-Fi, then create and edit a note. Confirm your
  changes sync when you reconnect.

  ## How to choose in five minutes


  Matching your needs to the best free note-taking apps usually comes down to a
  few questions:


  - **One ecosystem:** Apple Notes for Apple devices, or Google Keep for Google
  accounts.

  - **Mixed devices on a zero budget:** Obsidian with a folder-sync setup, or
  Notesnook.

  - **Students:** OneNote, as long as your files stay under 5 GB.

  - **Under 50 notes:** Evernote works for a short trial.

  - **Team pages:** Watch Notion's 1,000-block limit as soon as a second member
  joins.

  - **Wanting to leave later:** Pick an app that exports plain Markdown or PDF.


  At 3minread, our rule of thumb is simple: choose the app whose limit you won't
  hit, and run the export test before you commit.


  ## Frequently asked questions


  ### Is Evernote free?


  Yes, with limits. The free plan allows up to 50 notes and 1 notebook.


  ### What is the best free note-taking app overall?


  There isn't one winner. Obsidian is the strongest choice for local files and
  control, OneNote suits students, and Apple Notes or Google Keep work best
  inside a single ecosystem.


  ### Do I have to pay for Obsidian Sync?


  No. Sync is optional. You can sync a vault through a cloud folder service or
  Git instead.


  ### Is Notion better than OneNote for notes?


  For personal notes, OneNote is often the simpler choice on a free plan. Notion
  works better for pages a team will browse later.
