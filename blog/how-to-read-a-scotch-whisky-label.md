mk1: >-
  Knowing how to read a Scotch whisky label takes about a minute once you know
  the order of the fields. The age statement, the strength (ABV), the region,
  and a few optional terms such as non-chill filtered or natural colour tell you
  most of what the bottle is claiming. Some of those items are set by law, and
  others are simply the producer's own wording. Here's how we'd walk through a
  bottle on the shelf, field by field.


  ## Start With the Legal Minimums


  Scotch is a legally defined spirit. It must be matured in Scotland for at
  least three years, and it cannot be bottled below 40% ABV, according to [DLA
  Piper's summary of Scotch labelling
  rules](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/topics/whisky-law-insights/2024/scotch-whisky-labelling-and-bottling).
  Those two rules set the floor for every bottle you'll see, so a label doesn't
  need to prove them.


  The rules that govern the wording itself are in the [Scotch Whisky Regulations
  2009](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2009/2890). Keep that in mind as we
  go: a field is either controlled by law, or it's the producer's choice.


  ## Step 1: Find the Age Statement


  The age statement is the number most people look for first, such as 'Aged 12
  Years'. It tells you the age of the youngest whisky in the bottle, not the
  average.


  The [Scotch Whisky Association's bottler
  guidance](https://www.scotch-whisky.org.uk/media/2387/guidance-for-bottlers-and-producers_2025.pdf)
  uses a useful example. A blend that combines 8, 12 and 15-year-old Scotch can
  only show the youngest age, which is 8 years. The 12 and 15 year-old
  components are in there, but they don't appear on the label.


  ### What 'No Age Statement' Means


  A bottle marked NAS has no age statement. For Scotch, that doesn't mean the
  whisky is young or old. It still has to be at least three years old, and the
  producer may have blended in older or younger whisky without saying so. If you
  want a specific age, look for the number on the label rather than assuming.

  ## Step 2: Read the ABV


  ABV, or alcohol by volume, is the strength printed on the label, usually as a
  percentage. Most standard Scotch bottlings sit between 40% and 43%, which is
  where the legal floor meets common practice.


  Strength is more than a number on the front. Cask-strength bottlings come in
  much higher, and a 57.3% cask-strength example is still legal Scotch. The
  higher the strength, the less water has been added before bottling.


  Maturation also changes the liquid. The 'angel's share' is the portion lost to
  evaporation, roughly 2% a year according to [DLA
  Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/topics/whisky-law-insights/2024/scotch-whisky-labelling-and-bottling).
  That's why a cask ends up with less liquid than it started with, and why
  producers choose a bottling strength after maturation rather than leaving the
  cask strength as it is.

  ## Step 3: Check Filtration and Colour


  These two terms cause the most confusion, so here's the difference.


  ### What Chill Filtration Is


  Chill filtration means the whisky is cooled to roughly 5 to 10 °C and passed
  through a fine filter. The main purpose is cosmetic: it stops the whisky from
  turning hazy when it's cold or when water or ice is added. The [Wikipedia
  entry on chill filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chill_filtering)
  explains the process in more detail.


  ### Non-Chill Filtered (NCF)


  A label that says non-chill filtered tells you the producer skipped that step.
  Those bottles can turn cloudy with water or ice, which is a normal sign that
  the whisky kept more of its natural character.


  Here's the catch. Many NCF bottlings are around 46% ABV, and that leads many
  readers to assume strength proves filtration. It doesn't. Strength is a clue,
  not a rule. A 46% bottle with no filtration statement tells you its strength
  and nothing more about how it was filtered. The only reliable answer is the
  producer's own wording, such as the word 'non-chill filtered' on the label or
  the producer's tasting notes.


  ### Natural Colour


  Scotch law allows only water and plain caramel colouring (E150a) as additions.
  The regulations don't define 'natural colour' as a label term, so treat it as
  the producer's statement about the bottle rather than a legal guarantee.


  ## Step 4: Decode the Region


  The Scotch whisky regions are a legal list. The Scotch Whisky Association's
  official [regions
  page](https://www.scotch-whisky.org.uk/discover-scotch/enjoying-scotch/scotch-whisky-regions/)
  names five: Campbeltown, Highland, Islay, Lowland and Speyside. A region name
  on a label is legally controlled, which means it tells you something you can
  check.


  Don't read too much into a region's flavour, though. Speyside is a legal
  region, but a distillery there can make heavily peated whisky and still label
  it Speyside. The region tells you where the spirit was made, not exactly how
  it tastes. If you want a clearer sense of style, our [Islay vs Speyside
  comparison](https://www.3minread.com/islay-vs-speyside-whisky) covers how the
  two regions differ in practice, and our [Islay whisky
  guide](https://www.3minread.com/islay-whisky-the-smokiest-peatiest-whisky-from-scotland)
  explains where the smoke comes from.

  ## Step 5: Look for the Distillation Year


  Some bottles show a distillation year, and the rule is strict. The year is
  allowed only if all the whisky in the bottle was distilled in that calendar
  year. It must also appear with a bottling year, a maturation period, or an
  age, so you can see how the date is being used. The [DLA Piper
  summary](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/topics/whisky-law-insights/2024/scotch-whisky-labelling-and-bottling)
  sets out the rule.


  A distillation year is a useful clue for a single cask or a limited release.
  It doesn't tell you the age of a blend unless the label also shows an age.

  ## A Quick Checklist


  - Check the age statement, and remember it refers to the youngest whisky.

  - Note the ABV, and remember that cask strength runs higher than standard
  bottlings.

  - Look for the filtration wording, and don't assume it from strength alone.

  - Confirm the region name against the five SWA regions.

  - Read the distillation year only with its bottling or maturation context.


  ## Frequently Asked Questions


  ### Does no age statement mean the whisky is young?


  Not necessarily. A Scotch with no age statement must still be at least three
  years old, but the label doesn't say how old it is. If age matters to you,
  look for a stated age or check the producer's details.


  ### Does 46% ABV mean a whisky is non-chill filtered?


  No. Many NCF bottlings sit near 46%, but strength alone doesn't settle the
  question. Look for the filtration statement or the producer's own description.


  ### Can a Speyside distillery make peated whisky?


  Yes. A distillery in Speyside can produce heavily peated whisky and still
  label it Speyside. The region name tells you where it was made, not how smoky
  it is.


  ### Does every Scotch show a distillation year?


  No. Showing a distillation year is optional. When it appears, it must follow
  the calendar-year rule and sit with a bottling year, maturation period, or
  age.


  ## Takeaway


  Read the label in this order: the age statement, the ABV, the filtration and
  colour wording, the region, and then the distillation year. Treat anything the
  law controls as a checkable fact, and treat the producer's wording as the
  producer's own claim. Once you know that split, the label does a lot more of
  the talking for you.
